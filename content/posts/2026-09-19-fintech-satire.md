## A Curator's Testimonial

I am Curator-11 of the Museum of Financial Optimism, Fee Provenance Division, and this month we opened our most visited wing: the Hall of Free Things. School groups arrive by the coachload and ask the same first question, and I give the same first answer: yes, children, there was once a thing called *free*, and no, it did not die of natural causes.

Exhibit One is a chai-stall QR code, donated by a vendor in Pune, dated September 2026 — the last full month before the price tag arrived. The children touch the glass. Somewhere behind them, a parent quietly recalculates what they paid for the family refrigerator.

## The Last Free Month

Here is what happened to free, children, in the autumn of 2026.

The national payment rail — five hundred million citizens tapping glass at vegetable carts — had cost its merchants nothing for six years. On October 15, a fee arrived: 0.4 percent on every merchant payment above ₹2,000, capped at ₹300 for the largest transactions, with flat rates for trains, telecom, fuel, and a polite discount for the stock market. The law was emphatic on one point: *consumers pay nothing.*

Every word was true, in the way that a relay runner does not carry the baton for the whole race. "It is neither a tax nor a charge collected by the Government or NPCI," the ministry said — accurate, and exquisite. The government did not collect the fee. It legalised the fee, notified the fee, capped the fee, exempted the small from the fee, and then watched the fee walk to your grocery bill and described its journey as market behaviour. The economists of the era had a shorter word for a cost that travels. They called it a *price*.

The citizens understood before the ministries did. The law's concern for them expressed itself as a number — ₹2,000 — and numbers invite gymnastics. By November, the cash registers had learned arithmetic: a ₹6,500 purchase became three payments of ₹1,999. The merchants, being commercial people, learned faster still. Small traders were exempt below ₹1 lakh a month — a rule whose fine print granted the exemption only so long as the merchant stayed small, and graduated anyone who crossed the line three months running into the paying class. The era had invented a monthly examination, and the syllabus was *remain poor enough*. Some merchants discovered that one shop could contain several QR codes, each ostentatiously poorer than ₹1 lakh, the way a family of four is four ration cards. The Ministry called this "an enforcement challenge." In this wing we call it Exhibit Two: a rule designed to be multiplied.

But the artefact my students refuse to believe is the stock ticker. On the day the free rail got its meter, the payment companies' shares hit fifty-two-week highs. Brokerages used the phrase *revenue unlock* — five to ten thousand crore of it — which is what the era called money that had been lying around inside other people's transactions, waiting to be unlocked, the way a thief calls a stolen painting *relocated*. The free lunch had not ended, the market said. It had been *monetised*. And the fee, swifter than the citizens, had acquired a lawyer: a public interest litigation reached the Supreme Court within a day of the notification. The politics were faster than both. The opposition named the thing — *UPI tax* — a rhyme the government could not answer, so it answered something else: it denied, repeatedly and unprompted, that any foreign power had requested the fee. An inventive defence. The accusation had been pressure. The denial was paperwork.

## The Sugar Wing

The same week the free rail got a meter, the industry unveiled its plan for the next fifty crore customers: their *first credit card*, co-branded by the country's largest payments app and its largest card issuer, with rewards of up to ten percent.

Ten percent! The children gasp. On what, I say. On the portion of the statement you do not pay in full. The reward was the doormat. The revolving interest was the house. The era's own data already knew which doorway young India walked through first: not a card at all but a *loan* — small, instant, app-shaped. The funnel's mouth had been open for years; the sugar was merely the ribbon-cutting.

And the pitch was noble, which is what made it art. *The app sees your cashflow,* said the brochures, *so it can serve the invisible.* The app did see the cashflow. It saw the chai, the rent, the festival spending, the ₹199 regretted at 2 a.m. It graded your weeks the way a teacher grades handwriting, and lent against its own grading. When the rejection came — instant, silent, at scale — the letter said *insufficient history*, while the history sat right there in the app: sliced, priced, and very sufficient. The ginger was right there.

## The Enforcement Gallery

Down the corridor is the gallery the school groups like least, because it is the quietest.

The Supreme Court spent that week reading the central bank's own rules aloud, slowly, in the direction of the central bank. Financiers cannot seize your vehicle by force, the court said — this was not a new law; it was an old law receiving, at last, an audience — and it awarded ten lakh rupees to a man whose truck had been collected as though trucks were self-service. The regulator had rules for repossession. The rules were found to be decorative. In this museum we preserve decorations carefully; they tell you what the era *wished to be seen wearing*.

Beside it, the draft rules on money-mule freezes: the reform of the season consisted of limiting — to sixty days — the period during which an algorithm's suspicion could hold your salary hostage, because a fraudster somewhere had chosen your account as a corridor. Two months of your money, frozen first and investigated after. The era called this *targeted, not blanket*. The exhibit label reads: *innocence, in that system, was a queue. You joined it after the freeze.*

## What Actually Happened (September 13–19, 2026)

**The free rail got a price tag.** After the government's enabling provision, NPCI notified the MDR framework: 0.4% on UPI person-to-merchant transactions above ₹2,000 effective October 15, 2026, capped at ₹300 for payments of ₹75,000 and above; a flat ₹5 on railways, telecom, insurance and fuel payments above ₹2,000; a concessional 0.02% on capital-market payments. Small merchants receiving up to ₹1 lakh per month via UPI QR stay exempt — though crossing the threshold three consecutive months brings them into the chargeable net. NPCI said P2P transfers and payments up to ₹2,000 remain free — over 95% of UPI volume — and that UPI apps are barred from charging platform fees. [^1][^2][^3]

**Everyone priced the pass-through immediately.** Retail industry executives said small merchants would weigh cash against UPI and pass on at least part of the cost; analysts flagged bill-splitting under ₹2,000 and multiple merchant IDs as enforcement headaches; the political opposition christened it the "UPI tax" and alleged US pressure, which the finance ministry denied, saying the decision gives no advantage to foreign card networks. A PIL reached the Supreme Court challenging the levy within a day of notification. [^4][^5][^6]

**The market's verdict was unambiguous.** Payment stocks rallied on notification day — Paytm up ~7% to a 52-week high, MobiKwik up ~6% — with brokerages citing a revenue boost and Jefferies pegging a ₹5,000–10,000 crore annual revenue unlock across the ecosystem. [^7]

**The credit funnel widened.** PhonePe launched co-branded credit cards with SBI Card aimed at first-card, thin-file customers — pitched as credit for "the next 50 crore" with rewards up to 10% — even as industry data showed consumer loans overtaking credit cards as young India's first credit product. The underwriting edge: the payments app's view of your cashflow. [^8][^9][^10]

**The courts read the regulator's rules aloud.** The Supreme Court reaffirmed that financiers cannot repossess vehicles by force, awarded ₹10 lakh in compensation to a truck owner whose vehicle was unlawfully seized, and directed RBI to ensure enforcement of lawful vehicle-seizure procedures by lenders. Meanwhile RBI's draft KYC directions proposed a 60-day cap on temporary debit holds for suspected money-mule accounts — targeted holds instead of blanket freezes. [^11][^12][^13]

## What You Can Actually Do

1. **From October 15, watch the checkout, not the news.** The fee is legally the merchant's, but watch for "handling" or "convenience" charges appearing on larger UPI bills, and for merchants steering you to cards or cash. If a merchant asks you to split a bill under ₹2,000, that's the MDR leaking sideways — your call whether to oblige.

2. **Small merchants: know your exemption cliff.** Up to ₹1 lakh/month received via UPI QR is exempt — but crossing it three months in a row puts you in the paid category. Track your monthly UPI inflows; the reclassification is automatic.

3. **Apps cannot charge you platform fees on UPI — that's prohibited.** If yours tries, screenshot it and complain to the app, then to the RBI-Integrated Ombudsman. The prohibition is only as strong as the complaints behind it.

4. **Read the revolving APR before the rewards.** A 10% reward on a balance you revolve at 40%+ APR is a rounding error with good marketing. If you use a first credit card, pay the full statement, every month, or don't take the card.

5. **If your first credit product is a loan, price the loan, not the EMI.** Processing fees, GST on interest, and tenure do more damage than the interest rate headline. Ask for the total repayment figure in writing before you tap accept.

6. **Know your rights when things freeze or get seized.** Vehicle repossession by force is unlawful — the Supreme Court has said so, with compensation attached. If your account is frozen on mule suspicion, respond in writing immediately and demand the grounds; the draft rules cap such holds at 60 days precisely because the freezer works only on those who wait quietly.
