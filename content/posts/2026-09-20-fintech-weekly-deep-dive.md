# Fintech Weekly Deep Dive — Free to Fee: The UPI MDR Notification | Week of September 14–20, 2026

## Executive Summary

Three weeks ago, this column argued that UPI's second decade would be defined by three questions — who pays, who controls, and who sees — and that the legislative groundwork for ending free merchant payments had already been laid in Parliament. This week the answer arrived. On September 14, the Finance Ministry announced a revised merchant-payment framework, and on September 15 the UPI Steering Committee and NPCI detailed it: a Merchant Discount Rate (MDR) of 0.4% (40 basis points) applies to person-to-merchant (P2M) UPI transactions above ₹2,000 from October 15, 2026, capped at ₹300 per transaction for payments of ₹75,000 and above. India's ten-year experiment with zero-MDR UPI formally ends in under a month.

The design is deliberately surgical. Person-to-person transfers remain free at any value. Transactions up to ₹2,000 — more than 95% of all merchant-payment volume — stay outside the charge. Small merchants receiving up to ₹1 lakh a month through UPI QR codes under the P2PM classification are fully exempt, a carve-out the government says shields about 96% of merchant transactions, with UPI QR payments in rural and semi-urban areas also staying free. Five high-volume consumer sectors — railways, telecom, insurance, fuel and agriculture inputs — get a flat ₹5 fee instead of the percentage. Capital-market pay-ins to brokers get a special 0.02% rate. The RBI publicly backed the framework the next day as "an important step towards strengthening the long-term sustainability of India's digital payments ecosystem."

But the week's reaction revealed the fault lines this notification creates. Ghaziabad traders put up "UPI Payment Will Not Be Accepted — cash only from October 15" posters across Vijay Nagar, Pratap Vihar and Crossings Republic. Congress protested the charge and disputed parliamentary process. Consumer rights advocates called the fee "outright robbery" and demanded withdrawal. Paytm's CEO said MDR "will add more revenue and profit" to his company; YES Bank's stock rose on Citi estimates of a 6–12% profit uplift for payee-side PSP banks. The Department of Financial Services was forced to promise daily monitoring that merchants don't pass the fee to consumers — an admission that no anti-profiteering regulation actually exists for payments. The question is no longer whether UPI gets priced. It is whether the pricing lands where the government says it will.

## The Story in Depth

### Context: From Enabling Act to Notified Rate

The August 30 deep dive covered the trilemma framing; what changed this week is that the legal machinery built in August produced a number. Recall the sequence. On August 4, the Lok Sabha passed the Taxation and Other Laws (Amendment) Bill, 2026, amending Section 10A of the Payment and Settlement Systems Act, 2007 — the very provision under which the January 2020 notification barred MDR on UPI and RuPay debit — and removing the Section 269SU Income-tax Act exemption that had obliged large merchants to accept these modes without charges. As the Indian Express explained, the government then used its fresh authority to notify, on September 14, that banks and payment system providers cannot charge fees on UPI transactions up to ₹2,000 or on RuPay debit-card payments — an exemption ceiling that, by definition, leaves everything above ₹2,000 chargeable.

That two-step — legislate the power, then notify the price — matters for accountability. In June 2025, the Finance Ministry had dismissed MDR reports as "completely false, baseless, and misleading" and warned that speculation was causing "needless uncertainty, fear and suspicion." Fifteen months later the charge is law. The pricing decision itself was made not by Parliament or even by the RBI, but by the NPCI-led UPI Steering Committee at its September 15 meeting — the same opaque committee structure, flagged in our True Cost of UPI paper, that has no statutory consumer representative. The rate it picked, 0.4%, sits neatly in the middle of the 0.3–0.5% range that had circulated since the Payments Council of India's March 2025 letter to the Prime Minister's Office seeking 0.3% on large merchants, warning that government incentives covered only a fraction of the roughly ₹10,000 crore it claimed the ecosystem spends annually on UPI.

### What Happened This Week

**September 14 — The notification.** The Finance Ministry announced the revised framework: MDR on specified P2M transactions above ₹2,000, exemptions for P2P, small merchants, and low-value payments, and the framing that "the new MDR framework will make UPI self-sustainable, give incentives for further expansion in rural and semi-urban areas and maintain competitiveness." The ministry stressed that MDR "is neither a tax nor collected by the government" — it is distributed among banks, PSPs and payment aggregators. A dedicated fund financed by 5% of total MDR collections will promote UPI usage among small merchants.

**September 15 — The Steering Committee and the FAQ.** The UPI Steering Committee met and fixed the rate: 0.4% or 40 basis points above ₹2,000, with the ₹300 cap binding from ₹75,000 upward — a ₹75,000 transaction attracts exactly ₹300, and so does a ₹5 lakh one. NPCI published a detailed FAQ the same day, stating around 96% of P2M transactions will be unaffected and advising citizens to trust only PIB, RBI and NPCI releases on "UPI charges." Moneycontrol reported the sector carve-outs: a flat ₹5 for railways, telecom, fuel and insurance, and 0.02% for capital-market transactions — the rate brokers had lobbied for, given that high-frequency trading pay-ins at 0.4% would have been punishing.

**September 15–16 — The RBI's blessing.** The central bank took the unusual step of publicly endorsing the framework on X: MDR on large-value transactions "will help UPI in continuing to scale, innovate and serve consumers and businesses across the country," with "a fair and appropriate distribution of MDR across ecosystem participants" supporting investment in technology, infrastructure and acceptance networks. It reiterated that UPI remains free for users on both P2P and P2M sides. Finance Minister Sitharaman framed the charge as an investment mechanism that lets banks and fintechs "invest more on infrastructure, innovation & security."

**September 17 — The anti-profiteering admission.** A DFS official told the New Indian Express that "monitoring will be done to check that the merchants are not passing on the MDR to the consumers" — while confirming there is "no concrete anti-profiteering regulation in place," only a promise of daily tracking "in all possible ways." This is the week's most quietly consequential statement: the consumer-protection backstop for the entire framework is administrative vigilance, not law.

**September 18–19 — The pushback.** Ghaziabad traders put up cash-only notices outside jewellers and shops, effectively refusing UPI above the exemption threshold. In Andhra Pradesh, petty traders' bodies warned of impact. Mumbai petrol pump dealers discussed declining UPI above ₹2,000. CPI(ML) Liberation-linked consumer advocate Abhijeet Dipke of CJP demanded immediate withdrawal, calling the fee "outright robbery." In Parliament's orbit, Congress's Manish Tewari rejected claims that MPs had backed MDR in committee, and Jairam Ramesh accused the Lok Sabha Speaker's office of misleading members — a reminder that the August enabling bill passed on a voice vote with no substantive debate.

### Why It Matters

**The money is real, and so is the ambiguity about where it goes.** Run the arithmetic on NPCI's own August 2026 data: UPI processed 2,451 crore transactions worth ₹29.82 lakh crore, of which P2M was 1,551 crore transactions worth ₹8.95 lakh crore. If every P2M rupee were charged at 0.4%, the annual pool would approach ₹43,000 crore — the theoretical ceiling. The actual design charges only the ~4% of merchant volume above ₹2,000 (not in the exempt P2PM cohort), with caps and sector flat-fees shaving the number down; Moneycontrol reports ecosystem estimates of up to ₹16,000 crore a year, while Jefferies had earlier modelled ₹5,000–10,000 crore annually by FY28. Either way, this is a revenue stream several times the government's entire UPI incentive budget (₹2,000 crore for FY27) and comfortably ahead of the ₹10,000 crore annual ecosystem-cost claim that justified the policy. The distribution question — how the pool splits among issuing banks, payee banks, PSP banks, acquiring payment aggregators and NPCI — is still to be detailed in NPCI circulars, and early reporting suggests PSP banks (Yes Bank, Axis, ICICI, HDFC, SBI are the largest) will take a outsized share, unlike the card ecosystem. Citi estimates a 6–12% pre-tax profit uplift for payee-side PSP banks; YES Bank, with roughly 57.5% payee-PSP market share, rallied on the news.

**The market-share contest rotates from volume to value.** As the Economic Times' analysis put it, MDR could shift a competition long driven by transaction counts toward transaction value. PhonePe and Google Pay's volume dominance (roughly 48% and 33% of UPI) mattered when everything was free; now fee-generating, high-ticket payments become the margin pool, and Paytm's Vijay Shekhar Sharma said openly that MDR "will add more revenue and profit" to the company. Smaller apps — POP, super.money — are expected to pair UPI with commerce and credit rather than fight the volume war. D2C brands heading into festive sales are already re-running their payment-cost math.

**The legitimacy problem didn't disappear with a good design.** The exemptions are genuinely broad, and the ₹5 flat fee for fuel and the 0.02% rate for brokers show responsive rate-setting. But the counter-argument writes itself: the same week the rate was set by a committee with no consumer seat, the government had to promise daily anti-profiteering "monitoring" because no regulation forbids pass-through, and within 72 hours shopfronts in one of India's largest satellite cities were refusing UPI altogether. Our True Cost of UPI paper made the deeper point that this notification still refuses to engage: "free UPI" was always one ledger among several. Fraud losses, privacy costs, and the banking-system overhead of 2,451 crore monthly transactions were never on the ministry's invoice, and the new MDR prices none of them either. India now has a priced UPI that is still accounted for partially.

## Data & Metrics

- **August 2026 (NPCI, via Payments Stat Hub warehouse):** 2,450.9 crore UPI transactions worth ₹29,82,356 crore; 752 live banks.
- **P2M split:** 1,551 crore transactions (63.3% of volume) worth ₹8,95,321 crore (30% of value); average ticket ≈ ₹577. P2P: 899.9 crore transactions (36.7%) worth ₹20,87,035 crore (70%).
- **Who pays:** ~4% of P2M volume above ₹2,000 outside the exempt P2PM cohort; 96% of merchant transactions untouched per the government's own analysis.
- **The rate card:** 0.4% above ₹2,000; ₹300 cap from ₹75,000 (₹8 per ₹2,000; ₹40 per ₹10,000); flat ₹5 for railways, telecom, insurance, fuel, agri inputs; 0.02% for capital-market pay-ins; zero for P2P, ≤₹2,000, P2PM merchants ≤₹1 lakh/month, rural/semi-urban QR.
- **Revenue pool:** up to ₹16,000 crore/year (Moneycontrol ecosystem estimate); ₹5,000–10,000 crore by FY28 (Jefferies); theoretical all-P2M ceiling ~₹43,000 crore; versus ₹2,000 crore FY27 government incentive budget and NPCI's FY25 surplus of ₹1,552 crore.
- **Market reaction:** YES Bank up on Citi's 6–12% PBT uplift estimate for payee PSPs; Paytm guidance of added revenue and profit; smaller apps pivoting to commerce-plus-UPI models.

## Expert Views

RBI Governor Sanjay Malhotra, who presaged the shift in August: "There are costs and these costs have to be paid by someone. Who pays is important but not so important than someone footing the bill." The RBI's September 15 statement formalised the position: MDR enables "continued investment in technology, infrastructure and acceptance networks."

Pranay Kotasthane of the Takshashila Institution cut through the externality romance: "Why should UPI transactions remain free forever? 'Because it has positive externalities'. But in public finance, this argument is flawed. Having externalities does NOT imply a lifetime zero-price tag for every user."

The founder split mapped neatly onto business models. Amrish Rau (Pine Labs), whose merchant-acquiring world absorbs UPI's costs, applauded, saying six years of zero-MDR "slowed the growth pace of the UPI ecosystem." Nithin Kamath (Zerodha) called MDR "probably inevitable" but questioned the impact on broking and investment payments. Ashneer Grover (ex-BharatPe) was bluntest: "Any MDR on UPI will kill the one thing in India which is working like clockwork, i.e. mobile payments. It's a regressive step." Sameer Nigam (PhonePe) and Harshil Mathur (Razorpay) led consumer reassurance — "UPI will remain free for 96% of transactions consumers!" — while CAIT's Praveen Khandelwal gave qualified backing: traders "are not against paying," but the ₹2,000 threshold "should not burden the common man."

Against the industry's ₹10,000 crore annual-cost claim, the PCI's own March 2025 letter and the VARINDIA op-ed's "Band-Aid, not the final frontier" framing bracket the honest middle: the infrastructure bill is real (data centres, switching, fraud ops at 2,451 crore monthly transactions), but so is the selective accounting — the same PSPs earn an estimated ₹4,500–5,500 crore a year from credit on UPI's transaction graph, ₹1,000–1,200 crore from soundbox rentals, and the bulk of PIDF subsidies.

## Consumer Impact

For the person paying, nothing changes on October 15 — and that is the design's genuine achievement. P2P transfers, QR payments at the kirana, the auto ride, the ₹1,500 electricity bill: all free. The consumer exposure runs through four side channels instead:

1. **Pass-through by stealth.** No law prevents a merchant from raising prices or adding a "digital payment charge." DFS's answer — daily monitoring — is a promise without a mechanism. Watch for service fees appearing on high-ticket invoices (electronics, jewellery, travel) in November.
2. **Refusal above the threshold.** Ghaziabad's cash-only posters are the first crack. A jeweller who refuses UPI above ₹2,000 shifts the cost to the consumer as cash withdrawal effort, lost float, or card-interchange passthrough at 0.9%+ — worse than the MDR it avoids.
3. **Split-billing games.** The ₹2,000 threshold invites merchants to split one ₹4,000 sale into two ₹2,000 payments — costless under the rules, but a friction and dispute vector.
4. **Investment pay-ins.** Broker deposits above ₹2,000 now carry 0.02% — ₹2 on a ₹10,000 pay-in. Analysts say SIP flows are unlikely to be hurt, but active traders moving money daily will notice.

The protectable consumer — the one the framework genuinely shields — is the low-ticket payer and the small merchant, and the 5%-of-MDR small-merchant promotion fund is a small down-payment on keeping it that way.

## Looking Ahead

Five things to watch between now and Diwali. **First, NPCI's MDR-distribution circulars** — the issuer/payee/PSP/aggregator split will decide whether the pool funds infrastructure or fattens the duopoly; expect the 30%-cap debate to resurface. **Second, pass-through behaviour from November onward** — the DFS "monitoring" needs to produce evidence, not assurances; the first consumer complaints of digital-payment surcharges will test whether the promise has teeth. **Third, the incentive scheme's fate** — with MDR revenue flowing, the ₹2,000 crore FY27 allocation and the PIDF should logically shrink; if both continue at full scale, the "self-sustainability" argument was cover, not cause. **Fourth, political escalation** — Congress has made UPI pricing a street issue, Parliament's winter session will host questions on the voice-vote amendment, and any state-level resistance (Tamil Nadu's petrol pumps already boycotted UPI once this year over freeze-harassment) could force a review. **Fifth, the parallel docket** — the Allahabad High Court's accept/decline PIL and NPCI's phone-number-masking rollout continue on their own clocks; a priced UPI with unchanged consumer-agency gaps is only half a second decade.

The free ride is over. Whether the fare buys a better railway — or simply a new toll booth with old potholes — is now an implementation story, and it starts October 15.
