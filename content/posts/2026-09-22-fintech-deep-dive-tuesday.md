# Fintech Deep Dive — Tuesday | September 22, 2026

Tuesday is the Buzz & Funding column, and this week the buzz has a shape: money is flowing readily to profitable lenders priced to clear, and freezing solid wherever the regulator hasn't drawn a line. In the September 15–22 window, one unicorn took a 20% haircut to get its IPO out the door, a payments company borrowed instead of raising, a digital gold startup watched its $100 million round evaporate and settled for ₹29 crore from its own investor, a freshly listed lender sold new shares at an 84% premium to its IPO price, and an earned-wage-access company is pitching a $50 million unicorn round while quietly plotting a pivot into broking. Five stories, one thesis: in 2026's Indian fintech, the asset quality story is over — the pricing story has begun.

## 1. Moneyview's IPO: a unicorn that priced to sell, not to celebrate

The week's biggest ticket is [Moneyview](https://www.reuters.com/world/india/accel-backed-moneyview-seeks-624-million-valuation-trimmed-india-ipo-2026-09-21/), which fixed a price band of ₹32–34 per share for a ₹1,092 crore IPO opening September 24, with listing expected October 1. At the upper band, the implied valuation is about ₹5,985 crore — [roughly 20% below the ~₹7,500 crore its 2022 primary round implied](https://timesofindia.indiatimes.com/business/india-business/moneyview-targets-rs-5985-cr-ipo-valuation-20-below-2022-round/articleshow/134395873.cms), and well under the unicorn-level private marks of over $1 billion that [Inc42](https://inc42.com/buzz/moneyview-sets-%E2%82%B932-%E2%82%B934-price-band-for-%E2%82%B91092-cr-ipo/) says the company achieved by 2024. Here is the part that should make readers pause before the word "caution": this is not a distressed markdown. Moneyview is profitable and accelerating. FY26 revenue was ₹3,351 crore, up 43%, with net profit of ₹243 crore; Q1 FY27 revenue hit ₹1,041 crore, up 50%, with profit of ₹174 crore — up 159% — per [Entrackr](https://entrackr.com/news/moneyview-fixes-ipo-price-band-at-rs-32-34-eyes-rs-1092-cr-12557626).

So why did the company cut its issue from the ₹1,500 crore fresh component it originally filed, plus a larger offer for sale, down to ₹750 crore fresh plus 10.05 crore OFS shares worth about ₹342 crore? The company's own explanation, as [Rediff reported](https://money.rediff.com/news/market/moneyview-cuts-ipo-size-to-rs-1-092-cr-amid-strong-profits/54735420260921), is that profits and internal cash generation reduced the need for a bigger raise. That is credible and, frankly, the more interesting signal: after a decade in which Indian fintechs raised as much as the market would bear, a lender is deliberately shrinking its public fundraise because the business funds itself.

Follow the money, though. The OFS is where Accel (the largest pre-IPO holder at ~21.9%), Tiger Global (~13.8%), Ribbit Capital (~10.2%) and both founders monetise — a partial exit for the funds that backed the platform's [14 crore registered users and 48 financial partners](https://www.cnbctv18.com/market/moneyview-ipo-price-band-set-subscription-opens-on-september-24-lot-size-19994431.htm). And the fresh-issue allocation tells you what the business is: [₹325 crore is earmarked for lending under default-loss-guarantee arrangements and ₹250 crore to capitalise Whizdm Finance, its NBFC arm](https://entrackr.com/news/moneyview-fixes-ipo-price-band-at-rs-32-34-eyes-rs-1092-cr-12557626). That is the shape of a digital lender first and a platform second. The consumer stake: retail investors get 35% of this issue, and retail is being invited to buy a lender whose loan book is the growth engine. DLG structures mean Moneyview — not the partner bank — absorbs first losses; that is genuinely better aligned than pure referral models, but it converts the IPO cheque directly into credit risk on the balance sheet.

## 2. BharatPe borrows its way to an IPO window that MDR just reopened

[BharatPe raised ₹135 crore in debt](https://www.medianama.com/2026/09/223-bharatpe-rs-135-crore-debt-funding-ipo) this week — 13,500 non-convertible debentures of ₹1 lakh each, led by InnoVen Capital with ₹80 crore, Neo Group with ₹40 crore and Alteria with ₹15 crore — months after a ₹90 crore debt round led by RevX Capital. The framing in the [Medianama](https://www.medianama.com/2026/09/223-bharatpe-rs-135-crore-debt-funding-ipo) report is the news: this is a company rejigging its payment aggregator business *ahead of a potential IPO*. Debt, not equity, is the classic pre-listing move — extend runway, tidy the balance sheet, and avoid selling equity at whatever the current mark happens to be.

The context around BharatPe is where this story gets big. PhonePe had put its listing plans on the back burner during the US–Iran war market volatility, but [Mint reports](https://www.medianama.com/2026/09/223-bharatpe-rs-135-crore-debt-funding-ipo) it is likely to restart the process and now targets a February–March 2027 listing — catalysed by the government's decision to bring back merchant discount rate on large UPI transactions. Recall the policy shift the week before: [MDR has been set at 0.4% for transactions above ₹2,000](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/mctech3-mdr-set-04-transactions-above-rs-2000-oracle-india-fidgf). For years, the honest pitch against payments IPOs was that the core product — UPI — earns nothing. That objection just died. A 0.4% take on every large merchant transaction is a real revenue line, and the queue of payments companies that can now write an IPO prospectus with a visible monetisation story just got long: PhonePe with its February–March target, BharatPe circling behind, and every payment aggregator in between.

The consumer-and-merchant caveat deserves saying plainly: MDR is charged to merchants, not users, but merchant fees find their way into prices. The 0.4% will be debated as a consumer cost in 2027's earnings calls; this column flagged it then and will track it now. What this week proves is that the *capital markets* have already priced the policy change — debt investors will fund payments companies into IPO windows they previously wouldn't touch.

## 3. Jar: the digital gold freeze, in one funding round

The sharpest regulatory signal of the week came disguised as a funding note. [Jar raised ₹29 crore](https://entrackr.com/exclusive/exclusive-jar-raises-small-fresh-funding-from-unitary-fund-12547515) in a Series B2 from a single investor — existing backer Unitary Fund, buying 1,70,589 CCPS at ₹1,700 a share. The round values the Bengaluru gold-savings startup at about ₹3,155 crore post-money, [up 23% from ₹2,565 crore](https://businessmatters.in/jar-funding-unitary-fund-rs-29-crore/). That up-round headline hides the real news: Jar had been negotiating a $100 million round with investors including WestBridge, and those talks stalled. The entire ₹29 crore came from the inside.

Why would outside money walk away from a profitable platform claiming [30 million-plus users and over ₹4,000 crore of turnover in five years](https://indianexpress.com/article/legal-news/karnataka-high-court-jar-gold-retail-digital-gold-investigation-cid-10564582/)? Because digital gold has no regulator that claims it. SEBI has warned that digital/e-gold trading is unregulated; RBI has flagged legality doubts; and this month the Karnataka High Court [refused to quash a BUDS Act police probe into Jar](https://indianexpress.com/article/legal-news/karnataka-high-court-jar-gold-retail-digital-gold-investigation-cid-10564582/) — the CID investigation into alleged unauthorised collection of public money continues. The chilling effect is visible at the majors: [GPay, PhonePe and Paytm have pulled back from promoting digital gold](https://app.dealroom.co/news/note/jar-raises-3m-series-b-extension-from-unitary-fund-at-a-higher-valuation), which had been the category's customer-acquisition engine.

Read this round as a consumer, because it is a consumer-protection story wearing a funding story's clothes. An insider up-round says the existing investor believes the asset is sound. A collapsed $100 million round says no new investor will underwrite a category whose legal foundation is contested in a police investigation. Jar is one of the better-run names — profitable for the past year, ₹60 million-plus raised to date, Tiger Global holding 9.6% — and even it can't raise. Until either SEBI, RBI or Parliament decides what digital gold actually is, every user's "gold" balance is a contract with an intermediary whose regulatory status is the subject of an active CID probe. That is not a reason to panic; it is a reason for the regulator to finally answer the question.

## 4. OnEMI/Kissht: the four-month IPO-to-preferential-issue speedrun

Four months after listing, [Kissht's parent OnEMI Technology Solutions approved a preferential issue of up to ₹832 crore](https://entrackr.com/news/kissht-parent-onemis-board-approves-rs-832-cr-fundraise-12546223) — 2.64 crore shares at ₹314.11 apiece to 34 non-promoter investors. The marquee names on the ticket: [Axis Mutual Fund, HDFC Mutual Fund, MIT, WhiteOak, 360 One, Groww Mutual Fund and Bandhan Mutual Fund](https://www.business-standard.com/markets/capital-market-news/board-of-onemi-technology-solutions-approves-capital-raising-of-rs-832-cr-126091800429_1.html). The price is the story: ₹314.11 is [about an 84% premium to the ₹171 IPO price](https://www.tradingview.com/news/moodys:83a1ffab956ee:0-onemi-technology-shares-fall-as-board-approves-rs-832-cr-preferential-issue) at which the company raised ₹926 crore just months ago. The stock fell roughly 3% on the announcement — and was still trading near ₹349, far above the issue price.

The stated logic is lender logic. [Founder-CEO Ranvir Singh said about 75% of the proceeds go into the NBFC subsidiary](https://www.financialexpress.com/business/news/kissht-eyes-multiple-rating-upgrades-with-rs-832-cr-preferential-issue/4342552), with the rest for technology, AI and general purposes — and the company is explicitly chasing multiple credit-rating upgrades over the next two years to cut borrowing costs, while holding guidance of over 15% credit-cost reduction. Equity capital into an NBFC is rating-upgrade fuel: it deepens capital adequacy ahead of the rating exercise, which lowers the cost of the debt that actually funds the loans.

Two readings. The benign one: this is what a healthy listed-lender funding ladder looks like — IPO in May, rating-driven equity raise in September, cheaper wholesale debt next year. The sceptical one: consumer lenders are structurally capital-hungry, and a company raising fresh equity 120 days after its IPO is telling you its lending ambitions outrun its internal accruals — the same 84%-premium round that rewards early buyers also dilutes the retail investors who subscribed in May. The EGM is October 14. Watch who actually funds it when the allotment list lands: mutual fund participation at a premium is a real endorsement, but it is also the institutional investors getting preferential pricing that the May retail applicant never had.

## 5. Refyne's unicorn pitch — and the EWA-to-broking pivot playbook

[Tiger Global-backed Refyne is looking to raise $50 million at unicorn valuation](https://www.moneycontrol.com/technology/tiger-global-backed-refyne-looks-to-raise-50-million-at-unicorn-valuation-may-enter-broking-article-14034906.html), Moneycontrol reported today, with the fresh capital earmarked largely for a potential entry into broking. The company has raised $106 million to date; its core product is earned wage access — letting employees of partner employers draw salary as it's earned rather than waiting for payday.

The pivot is the pattern to watch. EWA monetisation is structurally thin — small per-transaction fees on small advances — so the platform math only works if the wage-advance user becomes a customer for higher-margin products. Broking is the current expansion of choice across Indian fintech precisely because it converts a payments or credit user into an investment-account relationship. Refyne doing it from EWA has a sharper edge than most: its distribution *is* the employer payroll, which means acquisition cost near zero and a user whose income is already verified. The consumer question is the old one about every salary-linked product: when the same entity advances your wages, sells you investments, and knows your pay cycle, the conflict-of-interest surface grows with every added product. Regulation of EWA in India remains a grey zone — neither deposit-taking nor lending in the classic sense — which is exactly why it keeps attracting capital that other credit categories can't.

The rest of the week's ledger, briefly: [Flam, an AI interactive-content platform, raised a $40 million Series B led by QED Investors](https://www.vccircle.com/qedinvestors-leads-series-b-in-ai-content-platform-flam) — a fintech-native fund backing a non-fintech company, with Shah Rukh Khan on the ticket; category lines in venture are blurring when fintech money chases AI content. [PB Fintech's board approved buying out the remaining stake in MyLoancare for ₹5 crore](https://scanx.trade/stock-market-news/companies/pb-fintech-approves-5-crore-acquisition-remaining-stake-myloancare/51126266) — small money, but it tightens Policybazaar's grip on its lending distribution. [Bessemer-backed TransBnk (rebranded TBX) is in advanced talks for a Series C](https://www.vccircle.com/premium/digitalwirtschaft/premium/45) in treasury infrastructure — one of the few infrastructure deals moving at growth-stage scale. And per [Entrackr's weekly tally](https://entrackr.com/report/weekly-funding-report-weekly-funding-report/funding-and-acquisitions-in-indian-startups-this-week-sep-14-sep-19-12551215), early-stage startups raised just $21.8 million across 14 deals in the Sept 14–19 week while growth-stage saw a single deal — Flam's — at $40 million. Wealthtech has pulled in $317 million across 26 deals in eight months, with Sahi reportedly in talks for ~$80 million that would be the category's largest.

## The takeaway

Strip the week to its pricing logic and one market emerges. Public and quasi-public money (Moneyview's IPO, OnEMI's preferential issue, BharatPe's pre-IPO debt) is abundant for profitable lenders, priced honestly — sometimes at a discount to private marks — and fast. Private venture money is scarce at growth stage and frozen solid in any category awaiting regulatory definition. Digital gold is the cautionary exhibit, EWA the next candidate. For founders, the 2026 playbook is now explicit: either be profitable enough to price an IPO against internal cash flows, or be small enough to raise from insiders. The middle path — raising big on a regulatory assumption — is closed until the regulators start answering questions.
