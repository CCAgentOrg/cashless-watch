# Fintech Deep Dive — Thursday | September 24, 2026

## The week cross-border ambition met the licensing grind

Global Fintech Fest's applause has faded; what remains is paperwork — and this week the paperwork moved. PhonePe cleared the first regulatory gate for its first overseas market, India and Bhutan completed the neighbourhood's first fully two-way UPI corridor, Kazakhstan emerged as the newest candidate for the UPI map even as the Alipay+ route stays frozen by geopolitics, a listed remittance company won perpetual forex licences to take cross-border payments to India's small towns, and stablecoin infrastructure quietly raised institutional money to compete for the same corridors. Five stories that define the week.

## Story 1: PhonePe's UAE in-principle approval — from tourist QR to regulated operator

On Tuesday (September 22), PhonePe announced in-principle approval from the Central Bank of the UAE for two licences: Retail Payment Services and Card Schemes (RPSCS) and Stored Value Facilities (SVF), following completion of initial regulatory due diligence ([The Hindu](https://www.thehindu.com/business/phonepe-secures-uae-central-bank-approval-for-two-payment-licences/article71496967.ece); [Livemint](https://www.livemint.com/money/personal-finance/phonepe-uae-push-more-than-upi-first-overseas-licences-mean-retail-payment-services-card-schemes-stored-value-facilities-11790071679111.html)). Final approval and commercial launch remain ahead, with no timeline disclosed ([Indian Startup News](https://indianstartupnews.com/news/phonepe-gets-uae-central-banks-in-principle-approval-for-two-payment-licences-12564373)).

The strategic shift is worth spelling out. Today, PhonePe's UAE presence is a UPI extension: through a partnership with NPCI International, Indian travellers scan local QR codes at NEOPAY and Network International terminals ([The Hindu](https://www.thehindu.com/business/phonepe-secures-uae-central-bank-approval-for-two-payment-licences/article71496967.ece)). That model monetises inbound Indian footfall but nothing else. The new licences would make PhonePe a *locally regulated payments business* in its first overseas market — serving UAE residents, not just Indian visitors. The company says it plans to work with regional banks, licensed payment service providers and local technology vendors, and intends to support **Aani** (the UAE's instant-payment rail) and **Jaywan** (its domestic card scheme) — meaning PhonePe could end up operating inside the UAE's own rails rather than merely importing UPI ([CEO Insights Asia](https://www.ceoinsightsasia.com/news/phonepe-gets-uae-payment-licenses-to-support-aani-and-jaywan-nwid-14893.html); [Firstpost](https://www.firstpost.com/business/phonepe-uae-payment-licences-aani-jaywan-14047526.html)).

Two contexts sharpen the picture. First, the GFF arc: the UAE entry was announced at Global Fintech Fest barely two weeks ago; an in-principle approval landing this fast suggests the regulatory groundwork was long done — but in-principle is not permission, and UAE final authorisations have their own clocks. Second, the IPO: PhonePe postponed its listing earlier this year amid market volatility, and Reuters reported it had sought a valuation of roughly $9–10.5 billion ([TechNode Global/Reuters](https://technode.global/2026/09/22/phonepe-uae-in-principle-payment-licenses)). A regulated Gulf business is exactly the kind of international revenue line an IPO story needs — the UAE is home to the largest Indian diaspora in the region, and remittances-plus-merchant payments is the obvious wedge.

For consumers, the test is unchanged from every cross-border launch we have tracked: what does the landed rupee (or dirham) actually cost? A locally licensed operator faces UAE consumer-protection rules on disclosure, which is genuinely good news. But "in-principle" headlines deserve "final approval" scepticism until the licence is in hand.

## Story 2: Bhutan's Phase-II — the first fully two-way UPI border

On September 17 in Thimphu, External Affairs Minister S. Jaishankar and Bhutanese Prime Minister Tshering Tobgay launched **Phase-II of international UPI for Bhutanese citizens visiting India**, completing two-way UPI-based payment connectivity between the two countries ([ETV Bharat](https://www.etvbharat.com/en/international/two-way-upi-with-bhutan-marks-another-step-in-indias-digital-neighbourhood-drive-enn26091707112); [ANI](https://www.malaysiasun.com/news/279313697/eam-jaishankar-bhutan-pm-tobgay-launch-phase-ii-of-international-upi-for-bhutanese-visitors-to-india)). Jaishankar's own framing: "Together with the use of UPI in Bhutan, this completes two-way UPI-based digital payment connectivity between India and Bhutan."

The direction of travel is the story. Until now, virtually every UPI linkage — Bhutan, Nepal, Sri Lanka, Mauritius, Qatar — primarily served Indians paying abroad, with person-to-person remittance legs added selectively (Nepal via NCHL in June 2026, Greece via Eurobank in May 2026) ([Mint](https://www.livemint.com/money/personal-finance/travelling-abroad-full-list-foreign-countries-upi-facility-available-indian-tourists-11789827584131.html)). Phase-II inverts the flow: Bhutanese nationals can now use their domestic payment applications to pay Indian merchants. UPI stops being a one-way export of Indian convenience and becomes a shared bridge — closer in spirit to the UPI One World model that lets foreign visitors pay in India, but here built on a live bilateral corridor rather than a prepaid wallet ([ETV Bharat](https://www.etvbharat.com/en/international/two-way-upi-with-bhutan-marks-another-step-in-indias-digital-neighbourhood-drive-enn26091707112)).

Why this matters more than it headlines: India is Bhutan's largest economic partner, border movement is constant, and the transactions that define the relationship are small-value and high-frequency — taxis in Phuentsholing, restaurants in Thimphu, handicraft shops, hotel bills. These are precisely where card networks are least convenient and cash is most aggravating ([ETV Bharat](https://www.etvbharat.com/en/international/two-way-upi-with-bhutan-marks-another-step-in-indias-digital-neighbourhood-drive-enn26091707112)). Two-way connectivity also changes merchant economics on both sides: an Indian shopkeeper no longer loses a Bhutanese customer to a cash-only counter, and vice versa.

The open questions are the ones regulators rarely front-load: FX disclosure on small tickets (does a ₹300 momo purchase show the ngultrum-to-rupee rate before you tap?), dispute resolution across two payment systems, and whether the P2P remittance leg eventually opens for Bhutanese workers in India. Bhutan is small; that is the point. It is the live pilot for what "two-way" means operationally — and every other neighbour watching the corridor will copy whichever parts work.

## Story 3: Kazakhstan knocks, Alipay+ stalls — two models of going global

The map expansion story of the week is Kazakhstan. Speaking to foreign journalists in Mumbai on September 16, Sonal Asnani, head of Asia Pacific at NPCI International (NIPL), walked through the UPI playbook as Kazakhstan moves toward cross-border QR payments with India among **11 countries identified for potential cooperation** ([Astana Times](https://astanatimes.com/2026/09/kazakhstan-eyes-cross-border-qr-payments-as-indias-upi-offers-blueprint)). Kazakhstan launched its national Interbank Mobile-Payment System in July, and its QR base is real: 2.34 billion QR transactions worth $26.3 billion in January–July alone. Asnani's description of UPI — "It's not an application, it's not a card. It's a payment layer" — is also a sales pitch for theNIPL model: build the layer, let local banks and fintechs compete on top, and connect bilaterally where QR adoption already exists.

The contrast with the Alipay+ file is instructive. The idea of linking UPI to Ant International's Alipay+ network — which would put Indian travellers' UPI apps to work across a merchant footprint of 100+ markets — has circulated since Reuters first reported the talks in February ([MediaNama](https://www.medianama.com/2026/02/223-india-upi-alipay-cross-border-talks)). On September 4, Reuters reported Indian authorities have **stalled** the proposal over national security and data-privacy concerns, citing "political grounds" ([Inshorts/Reuters](https://inshorts.com/en/news/india-halts-china-linked-alipay--integration-with-upi-over-data-concerns--report-1788517554616)). Yet the idea refuses to die: PYMNTS' September 22 real-time-payments world map still framed UPI–Alipay+ as India's potential shortcut to broader international reach ([PYMNTS](https://www.pymnts.com/real-time-payments/2026/apac-links-domestic-payment-systems-to-reach-new-markets)).

Put the two together and India's expansion doctrine comes into focus. The bilateral model (Bhutan, UAE, Singapore, now Kazakhstan-in-waiting) is slow, sovereign and controllable — every corridor is a negotiated treaty of standards, data flows and settlement. The platform model (Alipay+) is fast and capital-light but hands the acceptance layer to a foreign network with its own data gravity — exactly what got Chinese apps blocked in India since 2020. NIPL's own numbers explain the patience: UPI processed 24.51 billion transactions in August and operates in 11 countries ([PIB](https://www.pib.gov.in/FactsheetDetails.aspx?Id=151001&reg=20&lang=8)), but cross-border UPI volume in FY2026 was under 0.01% of total UPI transactions ([IBTimes India](https://www.ibtimes.co.in/upi-gambit-how-indias-payment-revolution-goes-global-what-comes-next-905362)). The map is wide and the volume is thin — which is precisely why the government will not trade data-sovereignty red lines for acceptance economics, and why QR-native markets with existing infrastructure (Kazakhstan's case, per Asnani: "Wherever there is a high level of QR adoption... it becomes natural for us to collaborate") are the cheapest next moves ([Astana Times](https://astanatimes.com/2026/09/kazakhstan-eyes-cross-border-qr-payments-as-indias-upi-offers-blueprint)).

For travellers, one useful disclosure from the NIPL briefing: under UPI Global Acceptance, users are shown the transaction amount, exchange rate and applicable fees *before* completing a payment ([Astana Times](https://astanatimes.com/2026/09/kazakhstan-eyes-cross-border-qr-payments-as-indias-upi-offers-blueprint)). That pre-payment transparency is the standard every other cross-border product this week should be held to.

## Story 4: RNFI's perpetual forex licences — cross-border plumbing reaches the last mile

While the headlines went to QR maps, the Reserve Bank did something quieter and arguably more consequential for who *actually* moves India's cross-border money. On September 21, RNFI Services announced that the RBI has granted **perpetual validity** to the Authorised Dealer Category-II (AD-II) forex licence of its subsidiary RNFI Money, and expanded the licence's scope to trade remittances, Nostro accounts and the appointment of Forex Correspondents ([Economic Times](https://m.economictimes.com/industry/banking/finance/rnfi-money-gets-rbi-approval-to-undertake-cross-border-trade-remittances/articleshow/134392464.cms); [SMEStreet](https://smestreet.in/banking/rbi-grants-perpetual-validity-to-rnfi-moneys-forex-licence-expands-scope-to-msme-trade-remittances-12559699); [YourStory](https://yourstory.com/2026/09/startup-news-and-updates-daily-roundup-september-21-2026)). This follows the subsidiary's in-principle authorisation to operate as a Payment Aggregator–Physical (PA-P).

Perpetual validity sounds technical; it is a business-model sentence. AD-II licences historically came with renewal anxiety — and renewal risk is death for a thin-margin forex business planning long-term corridor investments. With permanence plus an expanded scope, RNFI Money can build a standing cross-border trade-payments business on its **network of more than 280,000 active retail agents**, explicitly targeting smaller exporters, importers and MSMEs outside the major cities ([YourStory](https://yourstory.com/2026/09/startup-news-and-updates-daily-roundup-september-21-2026); [IBS Intelligence](https://ibsintelligence.com/ibsi-news/rnfi-services-secures-rbi-approval-for-payment-aggregator-licence)).

Here is why a consumer-advocacy desk cares: the licensed cross-border funnel has been concentrating. The big five PA-CB licence holders (PayU, Paytm, Razorpay, Juspay, Skydo) dominate inward merchant flows, and their economics work best for digital-first exporters. But India's export economy is also a man with a handicraft unit in Moradabad and a trading company in Erode — sellers whose payments needs are physical, document-heavy and poorly served by card-rail aggregators. Last-mile AD-II licence holders with agent networks attack exactly that gap, the way AePS and domestic money transfer did for banking. The trade-off to watch is pricing disclosure at the agent counter: forex margins are where last-mile cross-border products historically hide their costs. Perpetual licences reduce risk; only transparency obligations reduce gouging.

## Story 5: dtcpay's $25M close — the stablecoin corridor money is still coming

On September 18, Singapore-based stablecoin payments firm dtcpay completed its **$25 million Series A**, adding Japan's SBI Group as strategic investor through SBI Ventures Asset and the SBI-NTU-Kyobo Digital Innovation Fund, alongside Genedant Capital and existing backer Kwee Liong Tek; Vertex Ventures Southeast Asia & India had led the initial tranche in April ([PRNewswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/apac/news-releases/dtcpay-welcomes-sbi-group-as-strategic-investor-extending-series-a-to-us25m-302881922.html); [CoinDesk](https://www.coindesk.com/markets/2026/09/18/sbi-group-backs-payments-firm-dtcpay-in-usd25-million-funding-round); [The Block](https://www.theblock.co/news/business/2026-09-18-dtcpay-series-a-sbi-group-415483)).

dtcpay is a useful proxy for the competitive threat shadowing every UPI corridor announcement. The company holds a Major Payment Institution licence from MAS, an Electronic Money Institution licence in Luxembourg covering the EEA, and registrations across Hong Kong, Australia, the US and Canada — and it sells exactly one promise: move value across borders, instantly, on stablecoin rails, with a Visa-linked card on the spending end ([Bloomingbit/Cointelegraph](https://en.bloomingbit.io/feed/news/120665); [Cryptonomist](https://en.cryptonomist.ch/2026/09/18/dtcpay-series-a-funding)). "We did not raise this round to sustain what we have built. We raised it to fundamentally change how money moves across borders," CEO Alice Liu said ([TradingView/Cointelegraph](https://www.tradingview.com/news/cointelegraph:b3ee13800094b:0-dtcpay-welcomes-sbi-group-as-strategic-investor-extending-series-a-to-25m)).

India's cross-border stack is not in that race — RBI's posture on private stablecoins remains firmly against, with the digital rupee as its chosen instrument — but Indian capital is adjacent: Vertex Ventures SEA & India anchored the round's first tranche ([The Block](https://www.theblock.co/news/business/2026-09-18-dtcpay-series-a-sbi-group-415483)). The strategic point for Indian consumers: every dollar of institutional money flowing into regulated stablecoin corridors is a dollar of competitive pressure on remittance pricing — the corridor where NRIs lose the most to fees and FX markups. UPI–PayNow and a future Zelle bridge address specific corridors; stablecoin networks are corridor-agnostic by design. The consumer question is not whether India bans them (it has, effectively) but whether corridor competition from jurisdictions that permit them forces transparency on the routes Indians actually use. Watch the Gulf–India and US–India remittance pricing over the next two quarters: if stablecoin corridors keep professionalising offshore, incumbent margins there will be the first to crack.

## The week in one line

The cross-border project advanced on every front this week — a licensed Gulf beachhead for PhonePe, a two-way border with Bhutan, a new QR candidate in Kazakhstan, perpetual forex plumbing for small-town exporters — while the Alipay+ freeze and stablecoin money elsewhere bookended the two forces that will decide its pace: geopolitics sets the map's edges, and cost transparency sets whether consumers actually use what's on it.
