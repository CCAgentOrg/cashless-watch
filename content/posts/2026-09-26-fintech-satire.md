## An Acquisition Note From the Registrar

I am Registrar-12 of the Museum of Financial Optimism, keeper of the Denial Collection: the wing that preserves only artefacts the era officially declared to be what they were not. Our acquisition policy is strict. An ordinary lie does not qualify; the era's lies were common. We collect the certified ones — the fee that was not a tax, the commission that was not a conflict, the loss that widened while it grew.

This week's consignment arrived in excellent condition: documented, sworn, and still denying.

---

## Exhibit One: The Not-A-Tax, Its Ledger and Its Festival

You will recall from the Hall of Free Things that the national rail acquired a meter in the autumn of 2026. This wing preserves what came after: the meter's own paperwork.

The paperwork began with an estate division. The fee — 0.4 per cent on merchant payments above two thousand rupees — was split like a will: forty parts to the banks that issue, thirty to the banks that acquire, twenty to the apps, ten to the apps' partner banks. Analysts valued the estate at ten to twenty thousand crore a year. The government had denied foreign pressure by press note the week before; this week the Finance Minister appeared in person to swear the arrangement was *purely professional* — the fee not a tax, not a cess, not a surcharge, its collections never to touch the treasury. We have preserved both oaths. In this wing we encourage the child's question: if a thing is not a tax, why must everyone keep swearing?

The assurance, meanwhile, arrived with its own police. Within the week the government was reported to be *weighing mechanisms to shield consumers* from the fee's pass-through — a cost that, by law, consumers did not pay. On top of the fee sat an eighteen per cent tax — a tax on the not-a-tax — relief from which was promptly placed under consideration. And the merchants too small to be registered for that tax paid it embedded, unable to claim a rupee of it back, in a fee most of them had been told they would never owe.

Then the geometry. The charge fell from 0.4 per cent to a flat three hundred rupees once the ticket crossed seventy-five thousand — the effective rate *declining* as the spending *grew*. Respectability, in that economy, was priced at wholesale. And the exemption exam — stay below one lakh a month — was indexed to nothing, so inflation graded it yearly, walking the corner store across the pass line at the speed of vegetables.

Two domestic details complete the exhibit. The borrower learned that repayment itself now carried a toll: five rupees, flat, on every loan instalment above two thousand routed through the rail. The cap, you see, capped the fee. Nothing capped the number of instalments. And the merchants held a funeral. In Madhya Pradesh they covered their QR codes in black cloth and asked for cash; nationwide they announced a *No UPI Day* for the second of October — Gandhi Jayanti, the birthday of the man printed on the currency they were requesting. One office-bearer of the Indore garment trade promised that if the fee stood, the machines would be immersed in water bodies. In 2047 we know how that ended: the immersion was kept. Each October our children carry paper QR codes down to the river, as their ancestors carried clay gods — a god of convenience, drowned by its own priesthood at the first sight of a price.

## Exhibit Two: Nine Funerals and a Commission

The insurance wing is quietest, and here the silence has arithmetic. Persistency — the share of policies that stay alive — had fallen in some houses to eight in a hundred at ten years. Read it the way the school groups do, slowly: of every ten long-term policies sold, nine died early. The commissions, meanwhile, were paid *first* — front-loaded, in the era's own vocabulary — which meant the seller was paid in full at the beginning of a promise whose ending was somebody else's decade.

When the regulator proposed, in a draft open for comments, that the agent's income should attend the policy's whole life — that payouts should depend on the policy *surviving* — the market delivered its verdict inside a day. The intermediaries lost a fifth to a third of their value; Policybazaar's parent fell to a 52-week low. A respected founder chose the occasion to preach his favourite sermon: that the greatest risk in any regulated business is the regulator. Note what the market declined to price: the nine funerals. It priced the tenth — its own. A former bank chairman, to be fair, prophesied that honest commissions would recover their losses within the decade. Everyone in this exhibit had a forecast. The only people without one were those who had paid the premiums.

## Exhibit Three: The Season of Listing

The third gallery holds the season's parade: the listing, when the infirm were dressed, displayed, and subscribed.

A lender for Middle India — fourteen crore registered users, the average borrower aged thirty-two, teachers among the counted — came to market at six thousand crore, and the crowd offered one point four bids for every share on the first morning. Three hundred and twenty-five crore of the public's fresh money was earmarked, in the prospectus's own words, for lending back to Middle India under *default-loss-guarantee arrangements*: a mechanism by which the defaults are pre-forgiven on paper so that the lending may continue in fact. Elsewhere on the parade ground, another lender's loss widened by 269 per cent in the very year its revenue rose sixteen — both numbers appearing in one sentence, without irony, as momentum.

Nor were the founders riding the parade. The early money was leaving it: five hundred and thirteen crore of a jewellery marketplace sold in bulk here, two hundred and two crore of a children's retailer there — whole stakes, changing hands in the same week the crowd was buying. The wise were selling the harvest. The crowd was buying the field, at harvest prices, with borrowed enthusiasm.

And the exchange itself — the house that sets the fees — held its own open house. The institutions bid nearly thirteen times over. Retail, entitled by rule to a third of the feast, bid one point four, and was served what the hungry had left. The school groups always ask the same question in this room: *why did retail keep coming?* And I give the registrar's answer: because the apps had arranged it all into a single queue — the salary that arrived early, the policy that died young, the instalments that paid a toll, and now the shares. One citizen. Three balances. A lifetime subscription.

## What Actually Happened (September 20–26, 2026)

**The not-a-tax acquired a ledger, a tax of its own, and a protest day.** The NPCI-led UPI & Services Steering Committee finalised how the 0.4% MDR — live October 15 on specified P2M UPI payments above ₹2,000, capped at ₹300 for ₹75,000+ — will be divided: 40% issuer banks, 30% acquirers, 20% UPI apps, 10% partner banks; Citi estimates a ₹10,000–20,600 crore annual pool with roughly a quarter flowing to apps. [^1] NPCI CEO Dilip Asbe said UPI costs banks about ₹21,000 crore a year and the revenue will be recycled into soundboxes and AI infrastructure. [^2] NPCI also told NBFCs that UPI loan repayments above ₹2,000 will attract a flat ₹5 fee. [^3] Finance Minister Nirmala Sitharaman rejected opposition claims of external pressure as "absolutely baseless", called the decision "purely professional", stressed the MDR is not a tax, cess or surcharge, and said 5% of collections will fund small-merchant promotion. [^4][^5][^6] NPCI's arithmetic: 75% of India's 6 crore digital merchants have never recorded a UPI transaction above ₹2,000, and only ~10% of UPI transaction value risks pass-through. [^7][^8] The government is meanwhile weighing checks to prevent merchants passing the fee to consumers. [^9] NPCI called 18%-GST-on-MDR fears "misplaced" for GST-registered merchants, who claim input credit — while merchants below the GST threshold pay it embedded with no recourse; a GST Council relief review is likely. [^10] On the streets, Madhya Pradesh traders observed a "No UPI Day" with QR codes covered in black cloth, and a nationwide No UPI Day is set for October 2, Gandhi Jayanti. [^11][^12]

**Insurance distribution faced its reckoning.** IRDAI's September 23 draft proposes cutting customer acquisition costs, tying distributor compensation to long-term persistency while capping early payouts, and phasing expense-of-management limits down to 12.5% for life insurers and 20% for general insurers; comments are open until October 25. The context it addresses: 10-year persistency in some insurers is as low as 8%. [^13] The market's verdict: PB Fintech fell as much as 30% to a 52-week low, Turtlemint around 20%, with Bernstein noting the commission cuts hurt Policybazaar's parent most. [^14] Nithin Kamath called it a reminder that "the biggest risk is regulatory risk"; former SBI chairman Dinesh Kumar Khara countered that distributor earnings would recover 20–25% as higher retention compounds. [^15][^16]

**The listing season rolled on.** Moneyview's ₹1,092-crore IPO was subscribed 1.44 times on day one (GMP ~42%) at a ₹6,000-crore valuation; ₹325 crore of fresh proceeds is earmarked for loan disbursals under default-loss-guarantee arrangements; the self-described Middle India lender counts 14.03 crore registered users with an average customer age around 32. [^17][^18] Navi disclosed that its FY26 net loss widened 269% to ₹466 crore even as operating revenue grew 16.2% to ₹2,981.7 crore. [^19] Venture capital headed the other direction: Accel India and 360 ONE sold over 4% of BlueStone for ₹513 crore, and TPG sold its entire 2.21% FirstCry stake for ₹202 crore, both in bulk deals on September 25. [^20][^21] NSE's ₹22,568-crore IPO closed 5.69x subscribed — QIBs 12.68x, non-institutional 6.55x, retail 1.39x — and listed September 24. [^22]

**Quiet plumbing under a loud week.** The RBI conducted at least $10 billion of FX swaps to drain record banking surplus liquidity. [^23] Earned-wage-access firm Refyne is in talks to raise ~$50 million at a $1 billion valuation and may enter broking. [^24] Apple Pay confirmed an October India debut starting with Axis Bank credit cards — a tokenised, card-rail product landing the same month UPI's merchant fee begins. [^25]

## What You Can Actually Do

1. **From October 15, police the bill, not the press conference.** The MDR is legally the merchant's, and the government itself is designing anti-pass-through checks — consumer complaints are the data that enforcement runs on. If "MDR", "handling" or "convenience" charges appear on UPI bills above ₹2,000, screenshot it, complain to the merchant in writing, then escalate to NPCI's grievance portal or the RBI-Integrated Ombudsman.

2. **Merchants steering you to cash or card for bills above ₹2,000 is the fee leaking sideways.** You're free to pay however you like, but a surcharge tacked onto your UPI payment is not allowed — complain. And on October 2, UPI keeps working; the protest is voluntary. Nobody is entitled to tell you the rail is "down".

3. **Price your EMI rail, not just your EMI.** Loan instalments above ₹2,000 paid via UPI will cost a flat ₹5 each from October 15. If your lender offers NACH/e-mandate autopay, compare the rails: ₹5 × 36 instalments is ₹180 for the privilege of repaying.

4. **Before any long-term policy, ask two numbers: the insurer's 10-year persistency and the surrender value in years 1–5.** If the agent changes the subject to "tax benefits", the subject was the answer. If you already hold a dying policy, compare paid-up value against surrender before stopping premiums — and file comments on IRDAI's draft before October 25, because the loudest voice in that consultation usually drafts the exceptions.

5. **In IPO season, read "Objects of the Issue" before the grey-market premium.** If fresh money funds loan disbursals under default-loss-guarantee arrangements, you are subscribing to credit risk with better marketing. If the offer-for-sale list reads like the investor register of the last decade, ask yourself why the wise are selling the week you're buying.

6. **Small merchants: track monthly UPI receipts against the ₹1 lakh line.** Crossing it three consecutive months moves you into the chargeable class automatically, and inflation is grading that exam yearly. Keep your QR statements — they are your evidence if the reclassification is wrong.
