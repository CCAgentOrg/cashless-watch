# The Great Clarification Machine

*Dispatch from the Ministry of Nearly Clear Things, New Delhi, Financial Year 2046.*

## Future headlines, recovered from the archive

- **2046: Court Asks What the Charge Is; Charge Keeps Its October 15 Appointment**
- **Retailers Cancel No-UPI Day After Assurance; QR Codes Return to Work Without Reading the Minutes**
- **Half of First-Time Loan Originations Now Come Through NBFCs; Nation Welcomes More Firsts**
- **Apple Pay Arrives With Tokenisation; Entry Still Requires the Right Card, Device and Door**

I run the Great Clarification Machine. Citizens ask what a charge is; it replies with *not*, *may* and *subject to terms*. On the 2026 UPI charge, it could list what the fee was not. The rest remained unclassified.

That week, the Supreme Court asked the government to explain the legal character of the new merchant discount rate, but did not stop the planned October 15 start. A question was delivered; the timetable remained untouched. Every citizen received **Your Query Has Been Forwarded to the Future**. [^1]

The charge was set at 0.4% for specified merchant payments above ₹2,000, with exceptions and caps. The customer was told not to worry: the merchant pays, and must not pass the MDR on as a separate charge. The Machine called this “responsibility transferred, anxiety retained.”

“Then why did the merchant say the price might change?” I asked.

“Because a price can rise without wearing a little badge that says MDR,” it replied. “Would you prefer the rise to be named *convenience*?”

That is how the future learned to distinguish a prohibited surcharge from a new shelf price. Both can leave the same hole in a household budget; only one arrives with a line item. So we archived the bill, not the press conference.

Then came the protest. Retailers had planned a “No UPI Day” for October 2. After assurances from the Finance Minister, associations called it off on September 30. The scheduled fee did not disappear with the protest; it kept its date. In the museum label this is called **Dialogue: the successful cancellation of a demonstration, not necessarily of the disagreement**. [^2]

Was the assurance a victory? The Machine says yes. Was the fee postponed? The Machine says no. Can both answers fit on one banner? It recommends a QR code.

The second wing is called *First Credit, Lasting Memory*. A TransUnion CIBIL–FIDC report put NBFCs’ share of new-to-credit loan originations at 50% in the quarter to June 2026, up from 24% in June 2016. That can mean wider access; it does not prove predatory lending or bias. Still, a first-time borrower has little repayment history with which to bargain. Buy-now-pay-later can dress debt as checkout furniture; an opaque score can decide who sees an offer. [^3]

In my reconstructed 2026, every app congratulates the citizen on being “credit visible.” The confetti falls over the APR. A fictional borrower tells the archive: “I thought I was choosing delivery, not building a credit history. The app called me eligible; no one could tell me which part of checkout became a loan.”

This is not proof that every NBFC loan is harmful; it is a reason to distinguish access from a good deal. RBI rules require a Key Facts Statement with the APR and loan terms, limit digital-lending data collection to what is necessary and consented to, and treat merchant-platform deferred-payment credit as reportable lending. A borrower deserves the lender’s name and a repayment schedule before the confetti. [^4]

Apple Pay launched in India on September 30. Apple says it uses a device-specific token and does not retain transaction information tied to the user; launch access required an eligible Axis Bank Visa or Mastercard credit card. That is a meaningful design claim, not proof all payment data is private. A new button is not universal choice if entry requires a particular card and device. The Machine filed this under **Progress, with eligibility criteria**. [^5]

> “My first loan made me visible to the bureau. I wish the bureau had been visible to me first.” — fictional first-time borrower, 2026

## The Real Threat Behind the Joke

| Satirical element | Real consumer concern |
|---|---|
| A fee the Machine cannot name | Consumers need clear authority, scope and complaint routes when charges change. |
| “Merchant pays” beside a rising shelf price | A surcharge ban does not freeze prices; keep itemised bills and distinguish MDR from repricing. |
| Credit confetti, pay-later and an unexplained score | Wider first-time lending makes transparent costs, fair assessment and meaningful consent essential. |
| Privacy progress behind a card-and-device gate | Tokenisation can reduce card-number exposure, but eligibility still limits access; attribute privacy claims. |

## What actually happened this week (September 26–October 3, 2026)

- On September 28, the Supreme Court questioned the MDR’s legal character but declined to halt its planned October 15 rollout. [^1]
- On September 30, retailers called off the October 2 “No UPI Day” after assurances from the Finance Minister; the rollout date stood. [^2]
- A TransUnion CIBIL–FIDC report put NBFCs’ share of new-to-credit originations at 50% in June 2026, up from 24% in June 2016. [^3]
- Apple Pay launched in India on September 30 for eligible Axis Bank Visa and Mastercard credit cards, with Apple describing its tokenisation and data practices. [^5]

## What you can do

1. **For UPI, distinguish person-to-person from merchant payments.** Before October 15, check the bill. If a separate “MDR” charge appears, keep the receipt and raise it with the seller and payment provider; the FAQ says merchants must not pass MDR on separately. [^6]
2. **Before a digital loan, ask for the Key Facts Statement.** Check the lender, APR, total repayment and instalments. Read data permissions and use the lender’s grievance channel if paperwork and app promises differ. [^4]
3. **Treat privacy claims as design details.** Check the required card/device, what the issuer and merchant see, and whether another payment method is available without penalty.

In 2046, the Machine finally answered my original question. “What is the fee?” it said. “A question for which someone else receives the money.” I asked who should keep the receipt. The machine went quiet. That is why I still have a job.
