# Fintech Deep Dive — Sunday | October 04, 2026

**Focus:** Sunday — Weekly Review: the most consequential Indian fintech and digital-finance developments.
**Coverage period:** September 28–October 4, 2026 (seven calendar dates; reporting checked through 8:37 a.m. IST on October 4).

## Executive summary

The week’s strongest signal was that financial access is scaling faster than the institutions that must make it fair and recoverable. The Supreme Court let the October 15 UPI merchant-discount framework proceed for now, while asking the government to explain its legal character; trader groups split over protest, and consumer survey respondents said they would switch payment methods if merchants passed the cost on. Separately, the RBI reportedly pressed banks to fix recurring complaint causes, while a newly analysed policing survey shows how sharply cybercrime victims’ confidence falls between reporting a crime and seeing it investigated. NBFCs now originate half of first-time borrowers’ loans, and public-market and funding figures show capital still flowing—but concentrated outside fintech.

## 1. UPI MDR: the court kept the clock running, not the argument settled

On September 28, the Supreme Court declined to stay the notified merchant discount rate (MDR) on specified high-value UPI merchant payments. It issued notice to the Union government, RBI and NPCI and sought affidavits within four weeks. The bench pressed the Centre on the legal character of the charge: if it is neither tax nor fee, what authority supports it? This is a live question in the challenge, not a finding that the framework is unlawful. The court’s refusal of interim relief means the October 15 start date remains in place unless a later order changes it; it does not endorse the policy or decide the petition’s merits. [The Hindu’s report of the hearing](https://www.thehindu.com/business/Economy/if-neither-tax-nor-fee-what-is-this-expropriation-supreme-court-asks-govt-on-upi-mdr-charges/article71518779.ece) records the questions and the procedural order.

Under the [Finance Ministry’s published framework](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2310586), person-to-person UPI remains free; merchant payments up to ₹2,000 and qualifying small merchants remain outside MDR. The standard rate is 0.4% on specified person-to-merchant payments above ₹2,000, capped at ₹300 on transactions of ₹75,000 or more; selected essential sectors face a flat ₹5, while capital-market payments have a 0.02% rate capped at ₹300. The Ministry says banks have been advised to prevent merchants passing MDR to customers and that roughly 96% of all P2M transactions will be unaffected. Those are official design claims, not yet an observed post-launch outcome. The [NPCI FAQ](https://www.npci.org.in/uploads/FA_Qs_Merchant_Discount_Rate_MDR_on_Select_UPI_P2_M_Transactions_58dba1d39e.pdf) is the operational companion; the court dispute now tests the legal basis and decision-making around the arrangement.

The October 2 protest also revealed that “the traders” are not a single bloc. AIMRA and AICPDF withdrew their proposed national “No UPI Day” after meeting the Finance Minister on September 30, but Mumbai’s Federation of Retail Traders Welfare Association and other groups continued to press their opposition. That is a withdrawal of one coordinated call, not evidence that merchants accepted the fee. A [LocalCircles survey reported by The Hindu](https://www.thehindu.com/news/cities/mumbai/four-in-five-mumbaikars-unwilling-to-bear-mdr-fee-on-upi-payments-above-2000-study/article71532059.ece) found only 21% of surveyed Mumbai residents would keep using UPI if a merchant passed the fee on; 29% said they would use credit cards, 20% cash and 17% debit cards. It also reported that, in a separate national survey of over 67,000 users, 14% would continue using UPI in that scenario. These are stated intentions, not a forecast or a measure of actual switching, but they show why the pass-through rule needs a visible, low-friction complaint route.

NPCI’s September data show 24.07 billion transactions worth ₹29.37 lakh crore, down 1.8% in volume and 1.5% in value from August. Average daily volume still rose 1.4% to 802 million, and year-on-year volume grew 23% ([NPCI statistics](https://www.npci.org.in/what-we-do/upi/product-statistics); [Entrackr’s report](https://entrackr.com/news/upi-transaction-volume-stays-above-24-bn-despite-18-m-o-m-dip-in-september-12611636)). The dip reflects September’s one fewer day; the MDR has not begun. After October 15, measure merchant absorption, price changes, payment steering and any prohibited surcharge separately.

## 2. Complaint handling: a new RBI nudge meets an old recovery gap

On October 2, *The Economic Times* reported that at a September meeting RBI asked banks to trace recurring complaints to systemic, process or operational causes and strengthen complaint handling. It cited more than 1.33 million Ombudsman complaints in FY2024–25, up 13.55% from the previous year. The account relies on people familiar with the meeting: this is not a published circular, deadline or compensation promise. Still, closing tickets without correcting a failed reversal, account block or disputed-transaction process can hide the same failure recurring. [Read the report](https://economictimes.indiatimes.com/industry/banking/finance/banking/rbi-asks-banks-to-tackle-repeat-customer-complaints-fix-gaps-in-grievance-systems/articleshow/134625937.cms).

In an October 1 analysis of the Status of Policing in India Report 2026, Lokniti-CSDS researcher Krishangi Sinha reported that 63% of surveyed cybercrime victims were dissatisfied with police handling; satisfaction fell from 50% among helpline users to 43% during investigation. Nearly one in four had to visit a police station five times or more (23% overall; 38% rural, 19% urban). The report itself was released September 24, outside this edition’s window; its 8,306 respondents across 16 States describe reported experience, not audited recovery rates or UPI-fraud counts. [The Hindu’s analysis](https://www.thehindu.com/news/national/satisfaction-with-police-remains-low-among-cybercrime-victims/article71528544.ece) details the findings.

These are different systems and datasets, so they should not be collapsed into one complaint statistic. Together, though, they expose the same design test: can a report trigger accountable action rather than another hand-off? Banks should publish repeat-complaint themes, internal-ombudsman referrals and resolution times, then show that fixes reduce recurrence. For cyber fraud, a customer needs a case owner and status across the bank, payment provider and police—not simply a helpline number and repeated instructions to report faster. RBI’s [Integrated Ombudsman Scheme FAQs](https://www.rbi.org.in/commonperson/english/scripts/FAQs.aspx?Id=3407) explain escalation after a regulated entity fails to respond within 30 days or gives an unsatisfactory response; eligibility and scope still apply. A complaint channel is a safeguard only if it leads somewhere.

## 3. NBFCs are becoming the first formal lender for more borrowers

A TransUnion CIBIL–Finance Industry Development Council study, reported on October 2, found that NBFCs accounted for 50% of new-to-credit originations in the three months ended June 2026, against 24% in the same period of 2016. “New-to-credit” means a first loan recorded by the retail credit bureau; it is not half of all Indian borrowers or all loan value. In the same quarter, NBFCs represented 43% of retail-loan originations by volume and 30% by value. Consumer-durable loans made up 30% of new-to-credit originations in January–June 2026, with personal loans at 12%. The report covered about 2,000 NBFCs. [The Economic Times’ report](https://economictimes.indiatimes.com/markets/stocks/news/share-of-new-to-credit-customers-rises-to-50-in-june-2026-from-24-in-june-2016-transunion-cibil-fidc-report/articleshow/134650782.cms) summarizes the findings.

The number is an access milestone and a supervision warning, not a verdict on loan quality. NBFCs’ reach can bring a first formal loan to people and smaller businesses underserved by banks; that can build a bureau record and widen future choices. But a digital checkout loan or app-originated personal loan can make borrowing frictionless before a new borrower has much experience comparing annualized cost, late fees or collection practices. The report’s headline measures who originated the first loan, not its affordability, suitability, delinquency or the borrower’s later access to cheaper credit. Those missing outcomes matter more than a record count.

The next useful dashboard would connect first-loan originations to effective interest rates, fees, repayment stress, repeat borrowing and complaint outcomes, disaggregated by product and borrower type. Lenders and credit bureaus should make those measures legible without turning access into a marketing claim. For consumers taking a first loan, the practical rule remains simple: compare the total rupee repayment and penalties, not just the instalment or instant approval; check whether the app identifies its regulated lender; and keep the loan agreement and grievance contact. Growth is valuable when the borrower keeps the option to understand, repay and challenge it.

## 4. Moneyview’s IPO gives the lending model a public-market test; funding totals need a fintech filter

Digital lender Moneyview made its market debut on October 1 after a roughly ₹1,092-crore IPO. The issue comprised ₹750 crore of fresh capital and about ₹342 crore of shares sold by existing holders. Entrackr reported that the stock opened at ₹55.61 on BSE and ₹55 on NSE against an issue price of ₹34—a premium of about 64%—after subscriptions exceeded 100 times. The fresh issue can finance the company; the offer-for-sale component is liquidity for existing shareholders. Keeping those distinct is important when headlines describe a listing as “capital raised.” [Entrackr’s listing report](https://entrackr.com/news/moneyview-shares-debut-at-64-premium-after-100x-ipo-subscription-12611654) gives the issue composition and opening prices.

The week’s broad startup-funding number is not a fintech tally. Entrackr counted $411.46 million across 24 startups between September 28 and October 3, versus $188.49 million the prior week. Five growth-stage deals accounted for $357 million; Simple Energy’s $182-million Series C and SiMa.ai’s $150-million round together explain most of that bucket. Fintech’s disclosed round was [Seeds Fincap’s Series B of more than ₹100 crore](https://inc42.com/buzz/seeds-fincap-raises-%E2%82%B9100-cr-to-scale-lending-tech-capabilities), led by the Michael & Susan Dell Foundation; no valuation was disclosed. Inc42 reports the company claimed FY26 disbursals of about ₹620 crore and AUM of ₹722 crore, and plans to expand markets, branches, technology and risk-management capacity. The difference between the $411-million ecosystem headline and the fintech-specific deal is material. [Entrackr’s weekly tally](https://entrackr.com/report/weekly-funding-report-weekly-funding-report/funding-and-acquisitions-in-indian-startups-this-week-sep-28-oct-3-12618694) details deal counts and sectors.

Operating results add a second lens. CoinSwitch reported FY26 revenue of ₹324.19 crore and adjusted EBITDA of ₹86.38 crore, compared with ₹1.65 crore in FY25; this is adjusted EBITDA, not statutory net profit, and operating costs still rose 37%. PayMe said September loan disbursals crossed ₹200 crore and set a FY27 target above ₹2,000 crore—company-reported scale, not evidence of repayment quality. Investors are increasingly able to price fintech businesses through public disclosures, but growth, adjusted profitability and borrower outcomes are different measures. A strong debut or disbursal record does not resolve whether customer economics are sustainable.

## 5. The guardrails move onto the app screen as RBI warns about connected risks

SEBI’s October 1 [Project Jagrook circular](https://www.sebi.gov.in/legal/circulars/oct-2026/display-of-investor-awareness-message-s-by-stock-brokers-on-their-trading-apps-and-websites-under-project-jagrook_104858.html) requires stockbrokers to display regulator-provided investor-awareness messages on websites and trading apps. From October 5–31, website display alongside existing risk disclosures is required while app display is voluntary. From November 1, messages must appear on website landing pages and rotate on trading-app landing pages with risk disclosures on alternate days. This is a modest interface intervention, but it places an explicit disclosure where investors make decisions rather than relying on a user to find a PDF later. Its effectiveness should be judged by visibility and comprehension, not just broker compliance screenshots.

In an October 3 speech, RBI Governor Sanjay Malhotra warned that prolonged stability can encourage risk-taking and leverage, and identified cyber risks compounded by AI among global vulnerabilities. He also said India’s banking system remained resilient and described no imminent stress; this was a stability assessment and warning, not a new rule or forecast of crisis. For fintech, the useful connection is operational: fast-growing payments and credit products depend on third parties, models and shared infrastructure, so one provider’s weak cyber controls can become a system problem. [The RBI’s full speech](https://www.rbi.org.in/scripts/BS_SpeechesView.aspx?Id=1582) makes that systemic framing explicit.

## What to watch next

The immediate test is October 15: the UPI MDR start, the court’s next procedural steps and whether promised consumer guidance makes it easy to identify and report an improper surcharge. For digital credit, watch whether new-to-credit growth is accompanied by public reporting on repayment and complaints. For cyber protection, measure what happens after a victim files—not how many portals have been launched. And for fintech investment, separate sector-specific deals and audited performance from the much larger startup-market headline.
