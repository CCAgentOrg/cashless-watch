# Fintech Deep Dive — Tuesday | October 06, 2026

**Focus:** Buzz & Funding — venture rounds, public-market exits, IPO pipelines and operating signals.

**Coverage period:** September 29–October 6, 2026 (seven-day window ending 08:30 IST).

## Executive summary

This week’s fintech capital story is not a return to indiscriminate growth funding. It is a split market: Moneyview’s strong stock-market debut and StockGro parent AssetGro’s confidential IPO filing show that investors will fund and price established platforms; Seeds Fincap’s ₹100-crore Series B backs a smaller lender expanding its physical footprint; and lenders PayMe and Indifi are being judged by disbursal growth and credit costs. On the digital-asset side, CoinSwitch reported adjusted EBITDA profitability, though that is not the same as net profit.

The headline funding totals need care. Inc42 counted $233.6 million across 16 startup deals from September 28 to October 2, but its table classified only $10.4 million as fintech—the Seeds Fincap round. For Q3, Inc42 put fintech funding at $249 million across 13 deals, down 11% year on year. Entrackr’s separate Q3 compilation put fintech at $487.7 million. These are different data series, not numbers to combine. The sharper signal is selectivity: capital is following visible scale, a route to public markets, or evidence that underwriting and costs can hold up.

## 1. Moneyview’s debut: demand is real, but the opening premium is not a verdict

Digital lender Moneyview listed on October 1 after a ₹1,092-crore IPO: ₹750 crore of fresh issue and roughly ₹342 crore through an offer for sale. [Inc42’s listing report](https://inc42.com/buzz/moneyview-makes-bumper-debut-lists-at-64-premium-on-the-bse/) says the shares opened at ₹55.60 on the BSE, 64% above the ₹34 issue price, and at ₹55 on the NSE, nearly 62% higher. The offer had been subscribed about 98 times; [Entrackr’s listing report](https://entrackr.com/news/moneyview-shares-debut-at-64-premium-after-100x-ipo-subscription-12611654) records the same debut. The IPO price band implied a value of about ₹5,985 crore; at the BSE opening price, the reported market capitalisation was ₹9,788.6 crore.

The distinction between primary and secondary capital matters. Fresh-issue proceeds go to the company; OFS proceeds go to selling shareholders. Moneyview had earmarked ₹325 crore to support loan disbursements under its default-loss-guarantee arrangements and ₹250 crore for investment in its NBFC subsidiary, Whizdm Finance, with the balance for general corporate purposes. The raise therefore adds balance-sheet firepower to a lender as well as giving existing investors a partial exit.

A 98-times subscription and 64% opening premium demonstrate demand at that moment, not a durable valuation or a guarantee that credit losses will stay contained. Consumers should watch whether fresh capital supports responsible loan growth, transparent pricing and service; investors must assess credit quality across a full cycle. Moneyview is a reference point for digital lenders seeking public equity, but listed reporting will be less forgiving than a private funding announcement.

## 2. Seeds Fincap’s ₹100-crore round backs branch-led lending, not app-only scale

On October 1, Seeds Fincap announced a Series B of more than ₹100 crore, led by the Michael & Susan Dell Foundation, with Z47, Lok Capital, Norinchukin Capital and Alteria Capital also participating. [YourStory’s report](https://yourstory.com/2026/10/seeds-fincap-rs-100-cr-series-b-round-michael-susan-dell-foundation) says the company plans to spend on new branches, field staff and risk-tracking tools as it expands into rural and semi-urban markets; [Inc42’s account](https://inc42.com/buzz/seeds-fincap-raises-%E2%82%B9100-cr-to-scale-lending-tech-capabilities/) describes its underwriting model and operating scale.

Seeds is an NBFC lending to individuals and small businesses, with reported loan sizes from ₹50,000 to ₹1 lakh. The company said it was profitable in both FY25 and FY26, disbursed around ₹620 crore during FY26 and had ₹722 crore of assets under management in August 2026. Its reported network stood at 164 branches and more than 70,000 active borrowers; it is targeting ₹1,000 crore in AUM by March 2027.

The round backs an unglamorous, capital-intensive strategy: branches, field staff and risk monitoring. Digital acquisition does not eliminate the cost of verifying borrowers, collecting repayments or handling hardship; physical distribution can widen access, but it adds operating costs and makes fair, auditable collection essential. Investor backing is a vote of confidence, not proof that portfolio quality will survive expansion. Watch AUM beside delinquency, write-offs and branch costs—not disbursements alone.

## 3. AssetGro’s confidential IPO filing puts the investing-app model on a public-market path

StockGro’s parent, AssetGro Fintech, pre-filed a draft red herring prospectus with SEBI through the confidential route on September 26, with the development reported by Inc42 on September 29. The proposed issue could raise ₹2,000–2,500 crore, including a fresh issue of about ₹800 crore and an offer for sale by existing investors; the company may revise the final size and structure. [Inc42’s report](https://inc42.com/buzz/stockgro-confidentially-files-ipo-papers-eyes-up-to-%E2%82%B92500-cr-issue/) says StockGro had raised about $110 million in equity and claimed more than 4.5 crore users.

This is a proposed IPO, not an approved offer or a confirmed valuation. Because the filing is confidential, the draft is not yet public; investors cannot yet assess revenue quality, customer acquisition costs, trading-linked economics, or the balance between genuine investing and engagement features. The headline ₹2,500-crore ceiling is a possible issue size, not company value; only the eventual price band and share count can establish an implied valuation.

For users, this is not just a share sale: the app shapes how people discover investments and act on market content. The eventual filing should disclose revenue, customer-acquisition costs, and how research is distinguished from regulated advice. The confidential process gives analysts a basis for judging those questions only after documents become public.

## 4. PayMe’s disbursals are accelerating; its IPO language is still an intention

On October 1, Noida-based lender PayMe said its monthly loan disbursals exceeded ₹200 crore in September, nearly twice the ₹100-crore mark it crossed earlier in 2026 and up from ₹120 crore in July. [Entrackr reported](https://entrackr.com/snippets/payme-nearly-doubles-monthly-disbursals-to-rs-200-cr-targets-rs-2000-cr-for-fy27-12612682) that PayMe disbursed more than ₹1,300 crore in FY26, is targeting over ₹2,000 crore of disbursals for FY27, and hopes to reach a monthly run-rate of ₹500 crore by the fiscal year’s end. It also said the company is preparing for an IPO in the coming year.

These are company targets and reported operating figures, not completed results. Nor are loan disbursements the same as revenue, profit, or outstanding loan-book size: a rupee lent and repaid can be lent again. The ₹2,000-crore FY27 target is an annual flow, while ₹500 crore a month is an exit run-rate. If achieved and sustained, that monthly pace would annualise to ₹6,000 crore, so the two targets describe different points on a growth curve and should not be presented as equivalent.

PayMe offers personal loans, salary advances and business loans, and says it uses AI-based underwriting. Faster originations can demonstrate distribution and demand, but they do not by themselves show whether borrowers can repay or whether defaults and collection costs are rising. The consumer-facing safeguards to watch are the actual lender named in each loan agreement, the total cost of credit, clear grievance channels and fair collections—especially if growth depends on several partner or group entities.

The IPO plan remains an intention, not evidence of a filed prospectus. If it advances, the prospectus should let investors test growth quality and identify the regulated lender behind the app.

## 5. Two FY26 profit signals—and why the accounting labels matter

On September 29, CoinSwitch reported that revenue rose 150% to ₹324.19 crore in FY26, while adjusted EBITDA reached ₹86.38 crore, from ₹1.65 crore in FY25; the company put its adjusted EBITDA margin at 27%. Operating costs increased 37% to ₹270.07 crore. [YourStory’s report](https://yourstory.com/2026/09/coinswitch-turns-profitable-fy26-revenue-jumps-150-percent) attributes the numbers to the company. This is a striking operating-leverage claim for a crypto-investing platform, but adjusted EBITDA is an adjusted operating measure, not statutory net profit or cash generated after every cost. It should not be shortened to “CoinSwitch made ₹86 crore profit.”

Indifi offers a different, filings-based credit signal. The MSME digital lender’s FY26 operating revenue rose just 3% to ₹371.1 crore, but it moved from a ₹45-crore loss in FY25 to a ₹5.2-crore profit. [Entrackr’s October 5 analysis](https://entrackr.com/fintrackr/indifi-posts-rs-371-cr-revenue-in-fy26-turns-profitable-12624850), drawing on Registrar of Companies filings, says impairment costs fell from ₹107.6 crore to ₹55.3 crore while total expenditure declined 12% to ₹376.8 crore. Finance costs—the price of funding the loan book—still rose to ₹147.4 crore.

These figures point to two different routes to profitability: CoinSwitch reports faster revenue growth than cost growth; Indifi’s return to profit is mainly associated with lower impairment and spending. Neither single-year result proves that profitability is durable. For Indifi, repeat performance depends on credit selection, collections and funding costs; for CoinSwitch, it depends on sustaining activity and revenue while absorbing compliance, security and market-cycle costs. Consumers should treat “profitable” as a prompt to ask *which* profit measure, over *what* period, and whether the business can repeat it.

## Trend analysis: selective capital, more scrutiny

Across the week, the capital signals form a ladder. Moneyview has already reached the public market; AssetGro is preparing to ask public investors for capital; Seeds secured growth equity; PayMe is describing scale and an IPO ambition; and Indifi’s filings show that lowering credit losses can matter as much as adding borrowers. The common thread is not fintech becoming uniformly profitable. It is a higher bar for explaining how capital turns into durable revenue, controlled risk and credible exits.

Q3 data reinforce the need for precision. Inc42 estimated $2.2 billion of startup funding overall and $249 million for fintech, down 11% year on year across 13 fintech deals. [Entrackr’s Q3 estimate](https://entrackr.com/report/quarterly-funding-report/indian-startups-raise-29-bn-in-q3-2026-as-ev-ai-drive-funding-12612860) was $2.9 billion overall and $487.7 million for fintech. Each outlet compiles its own deal set; the difference is too large to treat one as a minor rounding error, and the reports do not provide a shared definition that lets us reconcile them. Use one source consistently for trend comparisons, and read the other as an independent estimate.

## Watchlist

- Whether AssetGro makes its prospectus public, what the actual issue size is, and what the filed financials say about StockGro’s revenue model.
- Moneyview’s post-listing performance and how it deploys the fresh ₹750 crore, rather than the first-day premium alone.
- Seeds Fincap’s AUM growth alongside credit-quality and collection indicators.
- Whether PayMe’s disbursal growth and IPO plan translate into filings with transparent loan, loss and grievance data.
- Whether CoinSwitch’s adjusted operating gains and Indifi’s lower impairment persist into FY27.
