# Fintech Deep Dive — Saturday | October 10, 2026

**Coverage window: October 3–10, 2026 (IST).** Saturday’s consumer-rights lens asks a practical question: when a payment fee, bank restriction, investment risk or data-sharing tool changes, can people understand the change before it costs them money or control?

This week’s developments were not one new consumer-protection law. They were five tests of whether existing financial systems make rights usable: UPI’s proposed merchant fee remained in limbo; the RBI restricted withdrawals at two urban co-operative banks; SEBI ordered a colour-coded debt-risk disclosure; the RBI announced account-aggregator interoperability; and stockbrokers began a new investor-awareness display phase.

## 1. UPI MDR: the date is a consumer issue, even if the bill is addressed to merchants

An announced fee is scheduled to take effect on October 15 for specified UPI person-to-merchant transactions above ₹2,000. The reported framework uses a 0.4% MDR for eligible transactions, with category-specific treatment and exclusions; it is not a fee on person-to-person transfers. During October 8–9, merchant bodies and payment firms sought more time, with January 2027 reported as a possible revised start. On October 9, Moneycontrol reported that the NPCI UPI Steering Committee meeting expected to consider postponement was called off. As of the end of this coverage window, there was no confirmed change to the announced date. [NPCI’s FAQ](https://www.npci.org.in/uploads/FA_Qs_Merchant_Discount_Rate_MDR_on_Select_UPI_P2_M_Transactions_58dba1d39e.pdf) and the [latest reporting on the stalled decision](https://www.moneycontrol.com/technology/upi-mdr-decision-in-limbo-as-steering-committee-meeting-called-off-article-14048737.html) should be read together: a possible postponement is not a postponement until NPCI confirms it.

The consumer-rights question is not simply “who pays MDR?” The fee is levied on the merchant side, but merchants can respond through prices, payment acceptance or explicit add-on charges. The first two effects are difficult for a shopper to distinguish from ordinary pricing; the third is visible and should be itemised rather than disguised as a universal UPI rule. A retailer saying “UPI now costs everyone extra” would be overstating what the published framework says. The exclusions matter too: an eligible high-value merchant payment is not the same thing as a small QR payment, a transfer to a friend or a recurring UPI AutoPay mandate, which [Zee Business reports is exempt](https://www.zeebiz.com/personal-finance/news-upi-mdr-from-october-15-will-your-health-insurance-life-insurance-premium-sip-in-mutual-funds-or-ott-netflix-zee5-prime-video-hotstar-payments-get-costlier-403540).

The governance problem is timing and legibility. Businesses are being asked to configure acceptance, category codes and customer communications just days before a festive-season launch, while the very committee expected to decide on delay did not meet. That uncertainty gets pushed down the chain to staff and customers. Clear notice should specify the transaction category, threshold, amount, whether the payment is recurring, and whether any amount is charged to the buyer. Consumers should keep the itemised bill and the payment record if a separate fee appears. Do not cancel a UPI payment or approve a “refund” request that asks for a PIN; query the merchant and raise a dispute through the bank or app’s official channel instead.

## 2. Two co-operative banks: “insured” is not the same as “available today”

On October 8, the RBI imposed directions on Tiruvalla Urban Co-operative Bank in Kerala and Sri Mahatma Basaveshwar Co-operative Bank in Afzalpur, Karnataka. Effective from the close of business that day, both were barred from accepting fresh deposits and from allowing withdrawals from savings, current or other depositor accounts without RBI’s written approval. The restrictions are for six months, subject to review. The RBI said the directions do not, by themselves, cancel either bank’s licence; the banks continue operating within the restrictions. ([Tiruvalla order](https://www.rbi.org.in/Scripts/BS_PressReleaseDisplay.aspx?prid=63763); [Afzalpur order](https://www.rbi.org.in/Scripts/BS_PressReleaseDisplay.aspx?prid=63764).)

The RBI notice says eligible depositors may claim up to ₹5 lakh from DICGC in the same capacity and same right, after submitting willingness and undergoing verification. This is a safety net, not a promise that every account balance can be withdrawn immediately. DICGC aggregates a person’s savings, current, recurring and fixed deposits across branches of the same bank when held in the same capacity and right; principal and interest together count towards the ₹5 lakh ceiling. Splitting money among multiple accounts at that one bank does not multiply the cover. DICGC’s [FAQ on deposit insurance and claims](https://www.dicgc.org.in/FAQs) explains the aggregation rule and, for banks under All-Inclusive Directions, the claim-list and verification process.

That difference—insurance versus liquidity—is central to a depositor’s rights. A household may be within the insurance ceiling and still be unable to use its own funds while the claim process is prepared and verified. A household above the cap faces possible exposure beyond the insured amount; insurance does not make the uninsured balance risk-free. Depositors should check whether accounts are in the same bank legal entity, preserve passbooks and statements, and use the bank administrator’s official willingness process. The RBI’s notice also allows the bank to set off loans against deposits under stated conditions, so a borrower-depositor should not assume every loan and deposit will be treated as separate pots of cash.

The supervisory lesson is uncomfortable but important: deposit insurance limits harm; it does not prevent a liquidity crisis or substitute for accessible, timely communication. When a bank is restricted, customers need a plain-language statement of which transactions are blocked, what essential exceptions exist, how to submit a claim, what records to keep and how to check progress. The RBI release establishes the restriction and ceiling; the DICGC process determines when the insured portion becomes payable.

## 3. SEBI’s Credit Risk-o-Meter: useful warning label, not a safety certificate

SEBI’s October 7 circular introduced a mandatory, colour-coded Credit Risk-o-Meter for debt securities. The six levels map the existing standardized rating symbols from AAA to D and must appear in offer documents and specified advertising and online bond-platform materials. Exchanges and depositories have 45 days to put the systems in place. The rule covers listed and proposed-to-be-listed non-convertible securities, commercial paper, securitised debt instruments, security receipts, and structured or market-linked debentures. ([SEBI circular](https://www.sebi.gov.in/legal/circulars/oct-2026/introduction-of-credit-risk-o-meter-as-an-additional-disclosure-mechanism-for-debt-securities_105081.html); see also the [Financial Express report](https://www.financialexpress.com/market/sebi-introduces-a-credit-risk-o-meter-for-debt-securities-4355889).)

A colour scale can help a first-time investor compare instruments without deciphering rating-company notation. Requiring it on online bond platforms matters because those interfaces are where retail investors discover and compare bonds. The improvement is modest but concrete: risk information is brought closer to the “buy” decision rather than buried in a long offer document.

The label still answers only one question: how the instrument’s credit rating maps to a level of credit risk. It does not promise repayment, insure the investment, or explain how quickly an investor can sell. It is not a measure of price volatility, market liquidity, concentration risk or whether a bond suits someone’s needs. The circular’s own framing—an additional disclosure mechanism—should be preserved. A red or high-risk label is not a forecast of default; a low-risk colour is not a government guarantee. Investors still need the rating rationale, issuer’s financials, maturity, security and redemption terms.

This distinction deserves emphasis as online platforms compress complex products into attractive cards and comparisons. A visual meter can improve comprehension, but can also create false confidence if platforms present it as a single overall “safety score.” The consumer test is whether the meter is visible beside the yield and purchase button, explained in ordinary language, and accompanied by a reminder of what it omits. SEBI has standardized the signal; platforms must not turn that signal into a sales promise.

## 4. Account Aggregator interoperability: more choice, more responsibility for consent

The RBI’s October 7 policy statement announced interoperability among NBFC-Account Aggregators. Customers are to be able to access and share financial information across different Financial Information Providers through any NBFC-AA of their choice. The RBI also said SEBI-regulated depositories would be enabled to include bank-deposit information in a Consolidated Account Statement through the AA framework, bringing demat holdings and bank deposits together in one view. The measures are targeted for implementation by December 31, 2026. ([RBI policy statement](https://www.rbi.org.in/scripts/BS_PressReleaseDisplay.aspx?prid=63743); [Financial Express explainer](https://www.financialexpress.com/business/banking-finance-rbi-widens-cas-makes-account-aggregators-interoperable-4355931/).)

Interoperability could reduce the “wrong app” problem: a customer should not have to choose a particular account aggregator just because a data provider works with that one. A consolidated view may help people spot fragmented deposits, plan cash flow or share records when applying for a service. These are real convenience gains, but they are not the same as new consumer rights unless the choice and consent are meaningful in practice.

The RBI’s announcement is a policy direction, not a detailed user-interface specification. The safeguards that will matter at launch include a readable statement of which institutions and account types are included, the purpose and duration of a data request, the ability to refuse or withdraw consent, and a clear record of what was shared and with whom. Interoperability also creates more routes through which data can move; more routes increase the importance of authentication, security and complaint handling. A consolidated statement should not become a back door for continuous profiling or a condition for unrelated services.

For consumers, the sensible rule remains purpose-first sharing: read the consent screen, check the requesting entity and purpose, and do not treat a one-time request as permission for indefinite access. For the RBI and participating platforms, the task is to make those choices as visible as the convenience headline. The December deadline should be judged not only by whether systems connect, but by whether customers can understand and control the connection.

## 5. Project Jagrook moves from awareness campaign to app real estate

SEBI’s October 1 circular under Project Jagrook began its first display phase on October 5. Through October 31, brokers must display SEBI’s investor-awareness messages and risk disclosures on their websites; showing those messages in trading apps is voluntary during this initial period. From November 1, the specified messages and risk disclosures must appear on broker websites’ landing pages and, on alternate days, on trading-app landing pages. [The circular](https://www.sebi.gov.in/legal/circulars/oct-2026/display-of-investor-awareness-message-s-by-stock-brokers-on-their-trading-apps-and-websites-under-project-jagrook_104858.html) is a small operational rule with a consumer-facing purpose: put warnings where people make investment decisions, not only in regulator campaigns.

Placement matters. A risk message behind a menu, buried in onboarding or shown only after a customer has entered an order is formally present but practically weak. Landing-page placement can reach a user before a trade; alternate-day rotation can keep messages from becoming a permanent visual wallpaper. Yet an awareness message cannot compensate for confusing product design, high-pressure prompts, dark patterns or inadequate suitability checks. Nor does reading a warning transfer the platform’s responsibilities to the customer.

The first phase also creates a testable compliance question. From November, investors should be able to see the messages on the relevant landing pages without hunting for them. SEBI should make it easy to report missing, obscured or unreadable disclosures and publish what enforcement looks like. Brokers should disclose which message is being shown, keep it legible across screen sizes and ensure that the warning does not vanish beneath promotional banners.

## What consumers should watch next

Three questions will separate useful protection from paper compliance. First, will NPCI formally settle the MDR date and publish an unambiguous category and buyer-charge explanation before merchants must implement it? Second, will RBI and DICGC make the bank-claim process trackable and explain the gap between the ₹5 lakh insured ceiling and immediate access to money? Third, will SEBI’s risk meter and Jagrook messages be visible at the decision point, without being marketed as guarantees?

A practical rule for this week: keep the receipt, statement or consent record that proves what you were shown and what you authorised. For a disputed UPI charge, ask for an itemised bill and raise the case through official bank/app support. For a restricted-bank deposit, use the bank’s documented DICGC willingness process and track the claim; do not share account credentials with intermediaries. For a bond or data-sharing request, treat a coloured label or a one-tap consent as the start of a decision, not the whole decision.

Consumer protection is strongest when price, access, risk and data use are legible before consent. This week’s regulators took steps on all four, but the notices are not the outcome. The outcome is whether an ordinary customer can see the rule, understand its limits, challenge an error and get a remedy.
