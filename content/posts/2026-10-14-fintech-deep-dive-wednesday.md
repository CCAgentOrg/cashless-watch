# Fintech Deep Dive — Wednesday | October 14, 2026

**Focus:** Consumer fintech — credit, data portability, payments and insurance distribution

**Coverage period:** October 7–14, 2026

## Executive summary

This week’s consumer-fintech story is not a single app launch; it is a contest over who gets to use consumers’ financial data, who pays to move it, and who bears the cost of credit and payments. The RBI announced cross-platform interoperability for Account Aggregators and a broader consolidated view of deposits; industry participants are preparing a standard fee model for that data pipe. Meanwhile, a new urban-consumer survey finds that payment-app users are far more comfortable paying than borrowing, and digital credit uptake is strongest in Tier-2 cities. Two pricing debates reinforce the point: NPCI postponed the planned merchant fee on selected higher-value UPI purchases until after Diwali, while IRDAI is considering lower insurance-distribution commissions. These measures are not all final or immediately live. For consumers, the key questions are whether consent remains meaningful, the total cost is visible before borrowing or buying cover, and product access expands without turning trust into a marketing slogan.

## Five developments to watch

### 1. RBI moves Account Aggregators toward one interoperable network

On October 7, the RBI said NBFC-Account Aggregators (AAs) should become interoperable: a customer will be able to access and share information from different financial information providers through an AA of their choice. It also said SEBI-regulated depositories will be enabled to include bank-deposit information in Consolidated Account Statements (CAS) through AAs. The RBI expects both measures to be implemented by December 31, 2026.[^1]

The proposition is less fragmentation: customers could use an AA of their choice to share participating institutions’ data, while the expanded CAS could give demat investors a fuller view of deposits alongside securities. The statement sets a direction and deadline; it does not promise that every provider will be connected on day one.

But an expected implementation date is not a promise of a universal dashboard. Coverage and reliability will matter: can people identify the institution supplying each record, correct stale data, revoke consent and see a clear purpose before sharing? AAs transmit customer data under consent; they are not meant to become data warehouses. Wider access can improve portability, but it also makes financial data more valuable to lenders and sellers. The consent screen—not the architecture diagram—will determine whether choice is real.

### 2. Account Aggregator data gets a price tag, with the customer formally outside the bill

A second development concerns the cost of keeping that pipe running. Business Standard reported on October 13 that the AA ecosystem will adopt a standard pricing model from November 1, based on a Sahamati circular reviewed by the newspaper and people familiar with the matter. Financial-information users—often lenders requesting statements—will pay a standard fee through the AA. The money is divided among the data-holding institution, the AA and Sahamati, the network’s RBI-recognised self-regulatory organisation. The circular says participating entities cannot directly charge customers for sharing data.[^2]

For a benchmark seven-month bank statement, the reported fee is ₹5.88–₹6.55 per successfully shared account: ₹2.80 goes to the bank and ₹0.40 to Sahamati, with the balance going to the AA. Banks have so far maintained data-delivery systems without compensation; the new model aims to fund that work and reward dependable service. The reported circular links bank payment to data-delivery performance, with an 80% minimum monthly success rate starting December 1 and rising later.

The design puts the bill on the institution using the data, not the customer authorising it. That could fund reliable bank feeds and reduce reliance on risky password-sharing or uploaded PDFs. It also redistributes value: technology-service providers that brokered earlier deals may lose out.

“No direct customer charge” does not prove there will be no indirect cost; the reporting does not show whether these fees will affect loan rates. Watch whether the fee stays a business cost, consent remains separate from loan eligibility, and customers can see what was accessed and why.

### 3. Digital credit is available, but trust—not awareness—is the bottleneck

The “How Urban India Pays 2026” report, released on October 13 by Pahlé India Foundation in collaboration with Amazon Pay, provides a useful consumer snapshot. It says 75% of urban respondents had used at least one financial product—credit, insurance or investments—inside a payment app. Yet 88% were aware of digital credit while only 31% used it. Trust ratings in the same report fell from 4.11 out of 5 for app security and 3.98 for payments to 3.10 when an app offered credit. The report surveyed 5,149 consumers across 100 cities in 20 states.[^3]

The gap is more revealing than the “financial super-app” headline. While 52% prefer one app for payments, investments, insurance and credit, bundling can also create more opportunities to cross-sell from sensitive financial data.

Tier-2 cities lead the report’s measured credit categories: BNPL usage at 60%, credit applications at 41%, and a Digital Credit Inclusion Index score of 58.64, above Tier-1 (53.10) and Tier-3 (55.77). Coimbatore reported 70% instalment-financing use for electronics and appliances; Kochi’s BNPL use was 45%. These are survey results, not transaction totals. Because the urban-focused report was produced with a payments provider commercially interested in the category, treat it as directional—not a census or proof of affordability.

The most useful signal is the report’s own trust condition: 51.5% said they would borrow through their app if fraud protection were guaranteed. That is not a guarantee consumers can currently demand from a survey response. It is a challenge to providers: publish the lender’s identity, annualised cost, repayment dates, consequences of missed payments, and complaint path before the user accepts. Digital credit inclusion should be judged by suitable approvals and sustainable repayment, not by how many people clicked “activate.”

### 4. UPI merchant charges are delayed—not cancelled

On October 13, NPCI’s UPI and Services Steering Committee deferred the merchant discount rate (MDR) on selected person-to-merchant UPI transactions until immediately after Diwali, with a new date to be notified separately. The rollout had been scheduled for October 15. Diwali falls on November 8 this year, so the deferment protects merchants from a new cost during the peak festive-sales period, but leaves the eventual start date open.[^4]

This is not a blanket charge on every UPI transfer. Earlier coverage described a 0.4% rate on selected P2M transactions above ₹2,000, with categories and caps; NPCI’s delay notice did not restate the terms. MDR is charged to merchants, not directly debited from a consumer. A delayed MDR is not an immediate consumer surcharge.

The direct economics fall on merchants and payment providers. CNBC-TV18 quoted one adviser saying merchant profitability—not consumer demand—would take the immediate hit, while a payments executive argued fees could support investment in merchant services. These are stakeholder views, not settled outcomes: merchants may absorb the fee, change payment terms or pass some cost through prices. This week’s evidence does not show which response will dominate.

The delay itself is politically informative: a payment rule can be revised when its timing threatens a high-volume shopping season. But the policy debate remains about sustainability—who finances acceptance, fraud controls and uptime—versus UPI’s promise of predictable, low-friction payments. Consumers need a stable rulebook and clear notice if a fee ever reaches them at checkout; merchants need to know the true net settlement before accepting a payment method.

### 5. IRDAI’s commission rethink could reshape online insurance distribution

On October 13, MediaNama reported that IRDAI could introduce its proposed insurance-distribution commission caps as early as January 2027. This is not a final rule: the regulator’s draft consultation paper was issued in September, and the October report records an executive’s indication of a possible timeline. The proposals would reintroduce product-wide caps across life, health, motor and property insurance, alongside tighter limits on insurers’ total expenses; the expense-limit changes would have a five-year transition.[^5]

The case for reform is that higher distribution spending has not clearly translated into broader coverage. MediaNama, citing IRDAI figures, says private life insurers’ expenses rose to about 22% of premiums from 16% in FY2021, and private general insurers’ expenses to around 32% from 25% in FY2019. IRDAI’s view is that caps could reduce mis-selling and costs. The Insurance Brokers Association of India disputes the regulator’s interpretation, saying that some apparent commission growth reflects expenses reclassified after the 2023 rules were relaxed. It warns of large revenue declines for brokers. PB Fintech’s CEO has separately estimated a significant effect on the company’s revenue if general-insurance commissions fall sharply; that is a company scenario, not a forecast of consumer premiums.

For marketplace buyers, lower commissions do not automatically mean lower premiums. Caps might curb incentives to push high-commission products, but could also change platform economics—reducing service, introducing advice fees, or encouraging distributors to seek insurer licences. The consumer outcome depends on comparison quality and post-sale help.

Clear disclosure of premiums, exclusions, incentives and claims responsibility matters more than a ceiling alone. January is a possible, not settled, start date; the final text and transition terms will decide the impact.

## The week’s pattern

Across credit, payments and insurance, India’s consumer-fintech market is moving from “make the transaction digital” to “make the economics legible.” The RBI’s AA changes could give people more control over where their data goes, while the new fee model funds the pipes that move it. The survey suggests consumers may accept a payment app as a financial front door but remain cautious when that door opens to borrowing. NPCI’s deferment shows the sensitivity of transaction fees in a spending peak; IRDAI’s proposed commission caps expose how distribution incentives shape what insurance products get sold.

The measure of progress should be consumer outcomes: usable consent controls; reliable and correct data; credit with transparent total cost and fair collection; payments fees clearly allocated; and insurance chosen for coverage rather than commission. Adoption and transaction growth are inputs, not proof of consumer welfare.

## Data and watchlist

- **By December 31:** RBI’s expected deadline for AA interoperability and deposit information in CAS. Check actual participating AAs, banks and depositories, not just launch announcements.
- **From November 1:** reported standard AA pricing begins. Watch data-delivery success, fee transparency and whether lenders treat the fee as a business cost.
- **After Diwali:** NPCI is to notify the new MDR start date. Confirm the final categories, thresholds and caps before assuming the reported schedule is unchanged.
- **Insurance distribution:** look for IRDAI’s final commission and expense directions, how broker/platform service changes, and whether consumers see lower costs or simply a different fee model.
- **Digital credit:** test providers against the survey’s trust gap—especially lender identity, APR, repayment schedule, fraud handling and complaints—not app downloads or BNPL adoption alone.
