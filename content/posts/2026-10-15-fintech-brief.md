### 1. SEBI proposes a new SME IPO gate — with a lower ticket for investors

SEBI's October 14 consultation paper would replace paid-up-capital thresholds for SME listings with post-issue market-cap bands. Companies valued at up to ₹1,000 crore would have to list on the SME platform; those above ₹1,000 crore and up to ₹4,000 crore could choose it, subject to preparing consolidated accounts under Ind AS. The regulator also proposes lifting the profitability bar: average operating profit of at least ₹3 crore a year over the previous three financial years, rather than the current test of ₹1 crore in at least two of those years. These are proposals, not rules in force. [Business Standard's report](https://www.business-standard.com/markets/news/sebi-proposes-market-cap-based-sme-listing-norms-lower-application-size-126101400870_1.html) and [Reuters' summary](https://www.reuters.com/legal/government/india-proposes-small-firm-ipo-overhaul-expands-role-institutional-investors-2026-10-14/) describe the consultation.

There is an alternative route for firms that miss the profit test: under the proposal, at least 75% of the net offer would need to go to qualified institutional buyers (QIBs), or subscriptions would be refunded. That may let credible but loss-making or lumpy-growth businesses raise public capital, while shifting more of the screening burden to institutions. Whether that is a sound substitute for a profitability record depends on the quality of QIB due diligence and the final allocation rules.

For individual investors, SEBI proposes cutting the minimum SME IPO application from ₹2 lakh to ₹1 lakh. The minimum lot would be ₹25,000, with at least four lots per application; after listing, the trading lot would fall to one share. That distinction matters: smaller secondary-market trades would become easier, but the minimum IPO cheque would still be ₹1 lakh. Lower entry cost is welcome, though SME IPO risk—thin liquidity, volatile prices and limited disclosure history—does not disappear with a smaller ticket.

### 2. RevRag AI raises $2.5 million for financial-services agents

Mumbai-based RevRag AI raised a $2.5 million seed round led by Accion Ventures and Cedar Hill Capital, with Wyser and Venture Catalysts also participating, [ETEntrepreneur reported on October 14](https://entrepreneur.economictimes.indiatimes.com/news/funding/revrag-ai-raises-2-5-mn-seed-funding-led-by-accion-ventures-cedar-hill-capital/134933156). The company says it will use the money to build its product, deepen enterprise deployments and enter the US market. The report gives no post-money valuation.

RevRag builds multilingual AI agents that sit inside apps operated by banks, NBFCs, insurers and fintechs, guiding customers through onboarding, product selection, sales and support. The company says it works with more than 45 financial institutions, including PhonePe, Bajaj Finance, Razorpay and Moneyview; it also claims its platform can reduce customer drop-offs and increase revenue at up to 70% lower cost. Those are company-reported figures, not independently verified performance results.

The investment points to a practical shift in fintech: from acquiring users for apps to helping them complete a task once they arrive. But in financial services, a conversational agent is also a sales and support channel. Institutions will need clear consent, auditable records, reliable language support and a human escalation path—especially when an agent explains credit, insurance or investment products. Funding is a signal of investor interest, not proof that automated guidance is accurate or in customers' interests.

For context only, NPCI had already postponed the planned October 15 start of a 0.4% MDR on selected UPI merchant transactions above ₹2,000 until after Diwali, without setting a new date. That announcement was made on October 13, outside today's strict window; the old start date should not be treated as active.
