# Fintech Deep Dive — Thursday | October 15, 2026

## India's cross-border fintech story is moving from rails to resilience

**Focus:** International & Cross-Border

**Coverage period:** October 9–15, 2026 (seven calendar days, inclusive)

This week produced three distinct signals rather than a new UPI corridor: Juspay disclosed FY26 results alongside a push into overseas markets; the Reserve Bank of India tightened parts of the foreign-exchange market as the rupee weakened; and the IMF, World Bank Group and Bank of Thailand released a framework for fraud-resistant digital finance. Together, they show why “cross-border payments” cannot be reduced to a fast transfer button. A firm needs access to foreign markets, a working FX and settlement layer, and rules for handling fraud that crosses jurisdictions.

The evidence is uneven. Juspay’s latest numbers are company-reported and its overseas revenue is not separately quantified. The RBI measures concern currency-market hedging, not consumer remittance fees. The new fraud framework is a reference for policymakers, not an Indian regulation. Those distinctions are important: they separate real developments from the easy but misleading claim that every new payment announcement makes international money movement cheaper or safer.

## 1. Juspay’s international push is growing—but the overseas economics remain hidden

On October 13, Bengaluru-based payments technology company Juspay reported FY26 revenue of **US$75 million**, up about **30%** from US$61 million in FY25 on a constant-currency basis. It said revenue had more than trebled from US$39 million in FY24. The company also reported **400 million daily transactions**, annualised payment volume above **US$1 trillion**, and more than **500 enterprise and bank customers**. Adjusted EBITDA was positive at US$4 million after excluding non-cash employee stock-option costs; the company nevertheless reported a US$10 million net loss. Juspay said its India payments business was profitable, while international operations were gaining traction across Asia-Pacific, Europe, the United States, Latin America and the Middle East. These are the company’s own figures, published in its [October 13 release](https://www.prnewswire.com/apac/news-releases/juspay-strengthens-global-footprint-as-fy26-revenue-rises-30-to-75-million-302906545.html).

This is a useful marker of Indian fintech internationalisation because Juspay sells payment infrastructure to businesses, not a consumer remittance app. Its work sits in the orchestration and processing layer: helping merchants, banks and platforms connect to payment methods and operate transactions at scale. That is a potentially exportable capability. But a headline TPV of US$1 trillion is not revenue, and neither the transaction count nor the customer total reveals how much activity happens outside India. Juspay did not publish an international revenue share, market-by-market volumes, or the investment required to acquire and support those customers. “Global footprint” describes reach; it does not establish overseas profitability.

The results also expose the trade-off behind expansion. Positive adjusted EBITDA coexists with a net loss, with the company attributing pressure to investment in international operations and new AI products. That can be rational if new markets produce durable, repeat business—but it is not yet proof that the strategy is paying off. Enterprise payments are sticky, yet each country brings its own payment schemes, licensing partners, data rules, fraud patterns and settlement arrangements. A company can scale transaction processing while still spending heavily on local integrations and support.

For Indian fintech watchers, the right next questions are not just “How many countries?” They are: what share of revenue is international; what are the gross margins by market; which local regulated partners hold funds and settle transactions; and how are service failures, refunds and fraud disputes resolved across borders? The release gives a credible scale signal, not those answers. No new funding round or valuation was disclosed in this announcement.

## 2. RBI’s rupee defence puts the FX layer back in view

On October 10, the RBI announced measures to reduce pressure on the rupee. It would make a special dollar facility available to Indian Oil, Hindustan Petroleum and Bharat Petroleum for their daily foreign-currency needs, moving those purchases away from the spot market. It also required authorised foreign-exchange dealers to maintain a **20% foreign-exchange risk reserve** against certain rupee-buying derivative contracts used to hedge current-account transactions when the notional value exceeds **US$2 million**. Separately, the threshold for rupee derivatives customers could transact without establishing an underlying exposure was cut to **US$5 million from US$100 million**. The [RBI’s October 10 release](https://m.rbi.org.in/scripts/BS_PressReleaseDisplay.aspx?prid=63783) details both circulars; [CNBC’s report](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/10/india-unveils-tough-curbs-on-dollar-demand-to-defend-rupee.html) supplies the market context, including its report that the rupee had fallen more than 7% during 2026 amid oil-price and global-yield pressure.

These are foreign-exchange market measures, not a new rule on UPI, card payments, or ordinary small-value remittances. The dollar window is aimed at three state-owned oil companies. The reserve requirement applies to specified derivatives and large exposures. A family sending money abroad or receiving a small inward remittance should not infer from this announcement that their transfer fee has changed by 20%, or that their bank must apply a new levy. The direct mechanism is narrower: make some large currency hedges more expensive and moderate demand for dollars in the derivatives market.

It still belongs in a cross-border fintech review because every international payment has a currency-conversion leg somewhere in its chain. A smoother app cannot remove FX risk or the need to fund settlement in the destination currency. When the rupee is under pressure, treasury desks, payment aggregators and exporters have to manage the timing and cost of conversion; any change in wholesale-market liquidity can affect commercial pricing, even if it does not translate mechanically into a retail fee. That pass-through must be measured, not assumed.

The consumer-facing test is transparency. Providers should show the actual exchange rate, the fee, and the amount expected to arrive before confirmation. For exporters and small businesses, the operational test is whether their bank or payment provider explains which exposure documentation is required and how the new threshold affects hedging workflows. The RBI’s intervention signals that currency stability is a live constraint on the plumbing beneath cross-border fintech—not that digital remittances have suddenly become more expensive or less available.

## 3. Bangkok Blueprint makes cross-border fraud cooperation a payments issue

On October 14, the IMF and World Bank Group, working with the Bank of Thailand, released the **Bangkok Blueprint for Fraud-Resilient Financial Services** at their annual meetings. The framework sets out **12 policy considerations across five pillars**: strategic and legal foundations; prevention, detection, response and recovery across the fraud lifecycle; use of existing risk-management and enforcement tools; domestic and international cooperation; and consumer protection and resilience. The Bank of Thailand’s [announcement](https://www.bot.or.th/th/news-and-media/news/news-20261014.html) and the [IMF release](https://www.imf.org/en/news/articles/2026/10/14/pr26336-imf-and-wbg-release-bangkok-blueprint-to-combat-cyber-enabled-fraud-in-digital-finance) describe it as a high-level framework for countries, not a binding rulebook imposed on payment providers.

The cross-border point is practical. A scam can be initiated in one country, routed through accounts or platforms in another, and end with funds moved through a third. A domestic payment operator may be able to identify a suspicious transaction, but it cannot alone compel a foreign institution to preserve records, freeze funds, or return money. The Blueprint’s emphasis on cross-sector and international coordination recognises that fast domestic payment rails can make the movement of money quicker without making the recovery of stolen funds equally fast.

For India, the framework is a prompt to ask how international payment links, UPI acceptance abroad and overseas settlement partners handle a fraud claim that crosses the national boundary. Who receives the complaint? Which institution can pause a payout? How quickly can the relevant data be shared, under what legal authority, and with what limits on personal information? Who bears the loss if a receiving institution acts too late? A QR payment may feel like a domestic scan to the traveller, but the exchange rate, routing, settlement and dispute path can involve multiple entities.

The Blueprint does not answer those operational questions for India. Nor does a global framework itself improve a customer’s chance of recovering money. The value will depend on whether national agencies translate broad principles into clear duties, contact points, timelines and outcome reporting. Consumer safeguards must also avoid a familiar failure mode: asking users to report faster while leaving firms’ responsibilities vague. Prevention, fast inter-provider alerts, accessible complaint handling and meaningful remediation need to be designed as one process, rather than treated as separate projects.

## Trend analysis: cross-border capability is more than a corridor announcement

The week’s strongest signal is that the cross-border payment product is only one layer of a broader system. Juspay’s results show an Indian technology provider trying to sell payment operations across multiple markets. The RBI’s move shows that currency liquidity and hedging rules still shape the conditions in which those transactions settle. The Bangkok Blueprint highlights that fraud response must travel across institutions and borders as readily as the payment instruction does.

That is a more realistic scorecard than counting countries where a QR code is accepted. For each new market or route, consumers and merchants need four things: a visible all-in price and exchange rate; reliable completion and refund timelines; an identifiable regulated entity responsible for the transaction; and a dispute process that works when the other end is overseas. For business users, the parallel list includes predictable FX access, documented settlement timing, and clarity on which institution handles compliance and exceptions.

This week’s evidence is encouraging on infrastructure ambition, but thin on outcome data. Juspay’s global transaction scale is not broken down by country; the RBI measures are wholesale FX controls rather than retail remittance reforms; and the Blueprint is a high-level call for cooperation. None is evidence, by itself, of lower consumer costs or better cross-border recovery rates. The next round of reporting should follow actual prices, failure rates, and redress—not only new partnerships and aggregate volumes.

- **Juspay operating scale:** 400 million daily transactions; annualised TPV above US$1 trillion; 500+ enterprises and banks; company-reported.

- **RBI FX measure:** 20% reserve on specified rupee-buying derivatives above US$2 million notional; no direct retail-remittance fee change announced.
- **Bangkok Blueprint:** 12 considerations under five pillars; policy framework, not an Indian regulation.
- **Funding and valuation:** No new financing or valuation for Juspay was disclosed in the sources reviewed this week.

## Watchlist

1. **Juspay:** Future disclosures on overseas revenue, market-level volumes, customer concentration and the profitability of international operations.
2. **FX conditions:** How the RBI’s measures affect wholesale liquidity and hedging costs—and whether authorised dealers communicate any effects clearly to business customers. Do not assume a direct change to consumer transfer fees.
3. **Fraud resilience:** Whether India’s regulators and payment providers publish cross-border escalation contacts, response timelines, data-sharing safeguards and recovery outcomes consistent with the Blueprint’s direction.
4. **UPI exports:** Track signed agreements and live operating corridors separately from exploratory talks or licensing announcements. A domestic instant-payment system built with UPI-derived technology is not automatically a bilateral remittance link.
