# Fintech Deep Dive — Thursday | October 22, 2026

**Focus:** International & Cross-Border

**Coverage period:** October 16–22, 2026 (seven days, inclusive)

## Executive summary

This week’s clearest cross-border fintech signals were not a new consumer remittance corridor or a dramatic UPI rollout. They were four different attempts to build the surrounding machinery: HUMO and NPCI International discussed implementation steps for an Uzbekistan–India QR link; Bengaluru-based RupeeFlo wants to combine overseas investor onboarding with money movement through GIFT City; Singapore’s FlexM secured strategic Series A funding for expansion that includes India; and Punjab National Bank described both an upcoming foreign-bank agreement and a possible future role in cross-border remittances.

The common theme is that “international payments” is not one product. It can mean a tourist paying an overseas merchant, an investor funding an Indian account, a business paying suppliers in several currencies, or a bank arranging finance with an overseas partner. Each has different rules, exchange-rate costs, settlement paths and complaint routes. Across the announcements this week, the promise is clearer than the consumer-facing terms: go-live dates, fees, conversion rates and dispute procedures were generally not disclosed. That gap is where the next round of scrutiny belongs.

## 1. UZQR and UPI: a signed agreement enters the implementation phase

On October 16, [Uzbekistan’s national payment system HUMO reported](https://humocard.uz/en/press_center/news/humo-and-npci-international-discuss-next-steps-for-cross-border-qr-payments-between-uzbekistan-and-i/) that it and NPCI International had discussed implementation steps for connecting Uzbekistan’s UZQR with India’s UPI. Their development agreement was signed on August 27; this was progress toward implementation, not a service launch. The proposed use case is for Indian visitors to scan participating Uzbek merchants’ UZQR codes through a UPI-enabled app.

That is a meaningful move from diplomatic intent toward technical planning, but the distinction between “working on implementation” and “live” matters. The announcement does not name a launch date, participating banks or UPI apps, merchant coverage, transaction limits, or the settlement arrangement. Nor does it spell out the exchange rate, any markup, who displays the final rupee amount before confirmation, or how a customer obtains a refund after a failed or disputed purchase. A QR scan can make the front end familiar while leaving the harder questions—currency conversion, settlement and redress—distributed among several institutions.

The expected benefit is specific: payment acceptance for Indian visitors in Uzbekistan. The statement does not establish a general person-to-person remittance service, nor does it promise that Uzbek users can send money into India through the same interface. A visitor may experience it as “UPI abroad,” but operationally the merchant’s QR network, the visitor’s Indian bank and app, NIPL, and local acquiring or settlement partners all need to work together. The Indian customer should be able to see the amount charged in both currencies, identify the party responsible for a failed payment, and get a usable complaint route even after returning home.

For merchants, the relevant test is not simply whether an Indian app can scan a code. It is whether the merchant receives predictable settlement in the expected currency, how quickly reversals arrive, and what fees or exchange deductions apply. The HUMO announcement is evidence of progress on bilateral interoperability; it is not yet evidence of lower costs or routine usage. A public pilot with participating merchants, clear terms and service-level reporting would turn the agreement into something travellers and businesses can evaluate.

## 2. RupeeFlo wants to connect overseas investor onboarding to GIFT City payments

On October 16, [*The Hindu BusinessLine* reported](https://www.thehindubusinessline.com/money-and-banking/rupeeflo-bets-on-gift-city-targets-30-revenue-share-as-bengaluru-based-fintech-eyes-payments-play/article71591394.ece) that Bengaluru-based RupeeFlo plans to extend its digital onboarding and compliance services into payments for investors and institutions at GIFT City’s International Financial Services Centre. Founder Dharmendra Maurya said it had received in-principle authorisation for a payment service provider licence. The proposed service would let an overseas investor move funds through RupeeFlo’s infrastructure after onboarding.

The company’s founder Dharmendra Maurya told the paper that GIFT City institutions currently account for less than 5% of RupeeFlo’s revenue. He is targeting a 25–30% share over the next year, with a possible 40% if the payments business gains traction. RupeeFlo reported annual recurring revenue of about **US$1.5 million (approximately ₹14 crore)** and said it had facilitated more than **20,000 investors** through institutional partnerships. The company also says its current onboarding process can be completed in 24 hours, compared with 15–30 days for some account openings and 30–45 days for broking onboarding. Those time comparisons and the 24-hour claim are company-reported, not an independent service-quality audit.

This is investment-funding infrastructure, not a retail remittance app or live payment rail. The opportunity is to link identity checks, account opening and funding for NRIs, overseas citizens of India and other foreign investors. That could reduce hand-offs, while giving RupeeFlo a recurring revenue line beyond onboarding—provided its authorisation covers the service and institutions adopt it.

The risks are equally concrete. “In-principle” approval is not the same as a final permission to offer every proposed service. The scope of authorisation, the regulated entity handling the funds, safeguarding arrangements, permitted currencies, foreign-exchange pricing and responsibility for failed transfers will define what the product can actually do. The report does not publish a fee schedule or a live launch date. RupeeFlo’s growth targets are forecasts, not evidence that GIFT City investors have adopted the product. Watch for the authorisation’s final terms, named financial-institution partners, transaction volumes and the customer’s all-in cost—not just a larger share of company revenue.

## 3. FlexM’s Series A is strategic capital for regulated market entry

On October 16, Singapore-headquartered FlexM [announced Series A growth funding](https://www.einpresswire.com/article/950150944/flexm-secures-series-a-funding-to-scale-global-payments-platform) from 24 Hermes VCC. The investor is to take a **10% equity stake** and partner on planned payment and digital-asset operations in Dubai and India. The amount was not disclosed. The investor said FlexM holds payment licences in Singapore and Canada, with applications underway in the UAE and India; the partner will fund agreed licensing costs and provide working capital.

FlexM sells business-facing infrastructure, not a consumer wallet. Its release describes multi-currency accounts, domestic and international payments, bulk payments and API connections alongside onboarding, sanctions screening and transaction monitoring. That pairing reflects the real work of cross-border finance: licensed routes to move funds, counterparty checks and exception handling in each jurisdiction.

The strategic-investor element is worth noting because the company says the partner is expected to bring transaction volume and market access as well as capital. That is a potentially more useful growth package than money alone, but the release gives no investment value, commercial commitments, expected India launch date or named operating bank. FlexM’s forecast of **200% annual growth in global cross-border payment corridors** is the company’s own projection, not a verified outcome. The release is company-provided material, so its product and growth claims should be treated accordingly.

India’s presence in the plan is still conditional. Licence applications are not approvals, and a joint venture’s announcement does not itself establish that a service is available to Indian customers. Before a provider markets “global” payments, customers need to know which legal entity accepts their money, where funds are held, what exchange rate and fees apply, how long delivery takes, and which regulator or complaint channel can help if the transfer is delayed. FlexM’s raise is a signal that investors see value in cross-border payment and compliance infrastructure; the value to Indian users depends on licences and an operating product, neither of which was announced as complete.

## 4. PNB signals intent, not a new remittance product

In an October 18 [*Business Standard* interview](https://www.business-standard.com/companies/people/pnb-s-foreign-bank-mou-10-15-days-away-says-md-ceo-ashok-chandra-126101800569_1.html), Punjab National Bank chief Ashok Chandra said an MoU with an unnamed foreign bank to finance mergers and acquisitions was in final vetting and could be signed within 10–15 days. He disclosed no counterparty or deal size. He also said PNB could explore cross-border remittances and was connected to 11 countries through UPI, adding that resilience would support more tie-ups.

These are different kinds of cross-border activity: M&A financing is wholesale banking, not a consumer payment corridor; UPI-linked international acceptance is not automatically remittance. Chandra did not identify the 11 countries, distinguish acceptance from remittances, explain PNB’s role in settlement, or announce a live product. “Explore” is an intention.

Still, the comments are revealing. A large public-sector bank sees international UPI connectivity not only as a travel-payment feature but as a possible base for additional services. That could broaden customer access if it leads to well-defined corridors through licensed banks, with predictable fees and clear beneficiary delivery. The challenge is that a domestic instant-payment experience may set expectations the international chain cannot yet meet: the recipient may be paid through another local system, currency conversion may occur at a different point, and dispute resolution may require cooperation across institutions and jurisdictions.

The next facts that matter are operational. If the foreign-bank MoU is signed, PNB should disclose its scope and whether it creates a repeatable funding channel or only a case-by-case arrangement. If the bank develops remittances, it should identify supported corridors, eligible customers, pricing, limits, delivery times and complaint ownership. Until then, this is a useful statement of strategic direction—not evidence that a new remittance service is live.

## Trend analysis: the border is a chain of institutions

The four stories sketch a layered market: HUMO–NIPL is QR acceptance for tourists; RupeeFlo is trying to link onboarding and investment funding at GIFT City; FlexM is funding multi-market payments and compliance expansion, with Indian authorisation pending; and PNB is weighing future services around its international UPI links. They are related, not interchangeable products.

The consumer test is whether a user can understand and contest a transaction end to end: disclose the rate and total charge before authorisation; show local and rupee amounts; name the regulated complaint recipient; explain reversals; and give realistic delivery times. Merchants need settlement currency, deductions and chargeback terms. A familiar QR or integrated dashboard helps only if it does not obscure responsibility.

This week showed movement in partnerships, business models and capital, but no comparable figures for active users, volume, fees, failures or redress. Progress should be measured by whether customers can move money at a disclosed price, on time, and recover it when something goes wrong—not by country counts or network size.

## Data & watchlist

- **RupeeFlo:** approximately US$1.5 million ARR; more than 20,000 investors facilitated, according to the company; GIFT City revenue-share target of 25–30% over the next year, potentially 40% if payments scale.
- **FlexM:** 10% equity stake for 24 Hermes VCC; Series A amount undisclosed; India and UAE licence applications reported as underway.
- **PNB:** foreign-bank M&A-financing MoU expected in 10–15 days from October 18, but no counterparty or amount disclosed; remittances remain exploratory.
- **HUMO–NIPL:** August 27 agreement followed by implementation discussions reported October 16; no live date, price schedule or merchant list disclosed.

Watch for a public UZQR–UPI test and its customer terms; RupeeFlo’s final authorisation and the regulated entity that handles investor funds; FlexM’s Indian licence outcome and disclosed investment size; and whether PNB turns its exploratory remittance comment into a named, priced corridor. These are the milestones that will separate market access from a usable service.
