# Fintech Deep Dive — Thursday | October 29, 2026

**International & Cross-Border | Developments: October 22–29, 2026**

This week's cross-border signal is that India's payments stack is being consumed in two directions at once. Outward, UPI is no longer only an acceptance network for Indian tourists scanning QR codes abroad — Zimbabwe's central bank is in advanced talks to make UPI the backbone of an entire national payment system, with negotiations targeted to conclude by October 31. Inward-facing but equally structural, GIFT City continued its run as the sanctioned gateway for money leaving India: exporter-payments platform Skydo received its final IFSCA licence to build cross-border payment infrastructure at the International Financial Services Centre, and JioBlackRock launched its first GIFT City fund to route eligible Indian investors into developed-market equities. The scale behind these moves: UPI processed roughly 24.5 billion transactions in August 2026 across 752 banks, accounts for nearly half of global real-time payment transaction volume, and is already live in 11 foreign countries for acceptance and remittances, per the government's own factsheet ([PIB](https://www.pib.gov.in/FactsheetDetails.aspx?Id=150962&reg=48&lang=2); [The Global Economics](https://www.theglobaleconomics.com/2026/10/08/zimbabwe-india-speed-payments)).

## 1. Zimbabwe wants UPI as its national backbone — and the deadline is October 31

The most consequential development of the week is not a product launch but a negotiation. Zimbabwe's Reserve Bank governor John Mushayavanhu told Bloomberg that talks with NPCI International Payments Limited (NIPL) could conclude by October 31, covering the adoption of UPI as "the backbone of a new national payment system" ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-05/zimbabwe-central-bank-seeks-india-s-npci-deal-to-boost-digital-payments); [Herald](https://www.heraldonline.co.zw/rbz-to-introduce-a-new-platform-for-digital-payments)). The timing matters: October 31 is a deadline for concluding the agreement, not a launch date for the service — a distinction the state-run Herald repeated in its Sunday explainer this week, describing RBZ's "plan for a shared payment system to cut transaction costs" ([Herald/Sunday Mail](https://www.heraldonline.co.zw/our-plan-for-a-shared-payment-system-to-cut-transaction-costs)). The proposed platform would be shared infrastructure connecting banks, mobile-money operators and fintech companies — an account-to-account alternative to cards for low-value domestic transactions that Mushayavanhu says could cut costs, speed transfers, and eventually extend to cross-border payments ([Business Insider Africa](https://africa.businessinsider.com/local/markets/zimbabwe-turns-to-indias-payments-giant-to-cut-transfer-costs-and-speed-up-digital/db2mrjb)). Zimbabwe's regulator has already done the plumbing groundwork: a March circular forced banks and payment providers to align merchant QR acceptance across providers, explicitly to avoid siloed systems ([Business Insider Africa](https://africa.businessinsider.com/local/markets/zimbabwe-turns-to-indias-payments-giant-to-cut-transfer-costs-and-speed-up-digital/db2mrjb)).

This is the second model of UPI's globalisation, and it is worth being precise about the difference. Acceptance deals — the kind that let Indian tourists pay at Singapore's Resorts World Sentosa or the Eiffel Tower — export UPI's *user experience* while money still moves through local rails. The Zimbabwe negotiation, like earlier NIPL agreements with Namibia, Peru and Trinidad & Tobago, exports UPI's *architecture*: a sovereign, real-time, interoperable platform built and operated under the destination country's own regulatory jurisdiction ([Reuters, on T&T](https://www.reuters.com/world/india/india-payments-authority-inks-deal-develop-payments-system-trinidad-tobago-2024-09-27); [Vivekananda International Foundation](https://www.vifindia.org/2026/october/27/Africa-Now-01-15-october-2026)). Zimbabwe would be among the first African adopters after Namibia, which makes the October 31 milestone a template-setting moment for the continent — the Vivekananda International Foundation's Africa bulletin this fortnight flagged the Zimbabwe talks as part of a broader pattern of African countries looking at Indian digital public infrastructure ([VIF](https://www.vifindia.org/2026/october/27/Africa-Now-01-15-october-2026)).

For consumers, the honest read is that this is promise, not delivery. The savings figure that would matter most — how much a mobile-money transaction between providers should actually cost — has not been disclosed ([Business Insider Africa](https://africa.businessinsider.com/local/markets/zimbabwe-turns-to-indias-payments-giant-to-cut-transfer-costs-and-speed-up-digital/db2mrjb)). And a national switch built on UPI's design inherits UPI's governance questions along with its strengths: who bears fraud liability across a switch that connects banks and mobile-money operators, what the dispute-redress timelines are, and how transaction data is governed will all be decided by Zimbabwean rules, not Indian ones. Those details are where a payments revolution either serves its users or quietly re-creates the silos it was meant to replace.

## 2. Skydo's final IFSCA licence: the exporter stack moves to GIFT City

On October 27, cross-border payments platform Skydo received final authorisation from the International Financial Services Centres Authority (IFSCA) to operate as a Payment Service Provider at GIFT City, upgrading from the in-principle approval granted in May 2026 ([ETBFSI](https://bfsi.economictimes.indiatimes.com/news/fintech/skydo-gets-final-ifsca-licence-to-operate-as-psp-at-gift-city/135233230); [CIOL](https://www.ciol.com/news/skydo-gets-gift-city-license-to-expand-global-payment-services-12759867)). The licence lets Skydo build multi-currency accounts, merchant acquiring and collections, and payment infrastructure for exporters, investing platforms, trade finance companies and other businesses operating inside the IFSC ([ETBFSI](https://bfsi.economictimes.indiatimes.com/news/fintech/skydo-gets-final-ifsca-licence-to-operate-as-psp-at-gift-city/135233230)).

The strategic layer here is the "dual-stack" that is becoming the template for India's cross-border payments: RBI authorisation as a Payment Aggregator–Cross Border (PA-CB) for the domestic side of export collections, and an IFSCA PSP licence at GIFT City for the international settlement layer. Skydo, an RBI-authorised PA-CB, now holds both halves. The regulatory journey that produced this stack is only three years old — the RBI's PA-CB framework replaced the old OPGSP regime and forced every non-bank handling export or import payments to either obtain direct authorisation or exit the business ([Skydo explainer](https://www.skydo.com/blog/opgsp-vs-pa-cb-guidelines)). Jio Payment Solutions' September launch of cross-border collections for exporters under its own PA-CB authorisation shows how quickly large entrants are crowding into the same lane ([Economic Times](https://m.economictimes.com/industry/banking/finance/jio-payment-solutions-launches-cross-border-payments-to-connect-exporters-with-global-markets/articleshow/133967006.cms)).

For the businesses this actually serves — SaaS companies, freelancers, exporters of goods and services — licensing is not abstract. The PA-CB era means the platform moving your money is directly supervised by the regulator: settlement flows are monitored, FIRC/FIRA documentation is tied to EDPMS closure, and there is an accountable entity if things go wrong. The consumer discipline remains the same as ever: verify the platform's authorisation status before routing receivables through it, and read the FX and fee schedule rather than the marketing page — "no fees" platforms are usually paid in the spread, and the spread is a real cost that compounds on every invoice.

## 3. JioBlackRock's GIFT City fund: outbound investing gets a ₹-adjacent on-ramp

On October 26, Jio BlackRock International AMC (IFSC) launched the JioBlackRock Advantage World Equity Fund (IFSC), its first GIFT City product — an open-ended fund of funds that will invest 90–100% of its assets in BlackRock's Advantage World Equity Fund, a globally diversified active strategy spanning the United States, Europe and Japan ([Economic Times](https://economictimes.indiatimes.com/mf/mf-news/jio-blackrock-international-amc-ifsc-launches-jioblackrock-advantage-world-equity-fund-ifsc-its-first-gift-city-fund/articleshow/135208792.cms); [CNBC-TV18](https://www.cnbctv18.com/personal-finance/jioblackrock-gift-city-fund-targets-global-equities-entry-threshold-costs-20019913.htm)). The minimum initial investment is USD 5,000, and the scheme is registered under IFSCA's fund management regulations rather than SEBI's mutual fund framework ([CNBC-TV18](https://www.cnbctv18.com/personal-finance/jioblackrock-gift-city-fund-targets-global-equities-entry-threshold-costs-20019913.htm); [Free Press Journal](https://www.freepressjournal.in/business/jioblackrock-launches-global-equity-fund-through-gift-city)). The launch follows the JV's July plan to bring outbound products to GIFT City, and arrives as JioBlackRock's AUM reached $3.09 billion in the second quarter of FY27, up 57% year on year ([Dealroom](https://dealroom.co/news/165789-jioblackrock-opens-gift-city-global-equity-fund-with-5-000-minimum)).

The structural point is that GIFT City is becoming the default channel for Indian retail investors seeking overseas exposure — a third route alongside the old LRS-based direct investing and the restricted mutual-fund window. It comes with genuinely different consumer characteristics that anyone subscribing should understand before the "democratising global investing" framing does its work ([Economic Times](https://economictimes.indiatimes.com/mf/mf-news/jio-blackrock-international-amc-ifsc-launches-jioblackrock-advantage-world-equity-fund-ifsc-its-first-gift-city-fund/articleshow/135208792.cms)). First, the fund is denominated in US dollars: an Indian investor is taking currency risk in both directions, and the investment is funded through the Liberalised Remittance Scheme, where tax collection at source applies to the outward remittance. Second, this is an IFSCA-regulated scheme, not a SEBI mutual fund — disclosure norms, investor-grievance machinery and exit mechanics live under a different rulebook than the one most Indian mutual fund investors are used to. Third, a fund of funds carries two layers of product cost — the feeder's and the underlying BlackRock strategy's — which is exactly where fee opacity hides. None of this makes the product bad; it makes it *different*, and "different" deserves a slower reading than a familiar SIP.

## 4. FOMO Pay and BNY: the invisible half of the UPI corridor

On October 28, Singapore-based FOMO Pay announced a collaboration with BNY to adopt its 24/7 USD Book Transfers solution, becoming one of the first financial institutions globally to extend round-the-clock USD settlement to eligible payments ([FOMO Pay](https://www.fomopay.com/news-media/fomo-pay-collaborates-with-bny-to-enable-24-7-usd-settlement)). Why does this belong in an India-fintech brief? Because FOMO Pay is NIPL's partner in Singapore — the institution progressively enabling UPI acceptance across its merchant network, with Resorts World Sentosa's online booking already live since the partnership was announced at Global Fintech Fest in September ([PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/apac/news-releases/fomo-pay-and-npci-international-to-bring-upi-qr-acceptance-to-singapore-with-resorts-world-sentosa-as-first-merchant-partner-302872143.html); [Moneycontrol](https://www.moneycontrol.com/technology/npci-international-partners-fomo-pay-to-expand-upi-acceptance-across-singapore-article-14026018.html)).

Acceptance is the visible half of a cross-border corridor; settlement is the invisible half. When a tourist pays by UPI in Singapore, the merchant eventually needs to be made whole in local currency — and every hour the settlement leg sits idle between cut-off times, somebody is holding float risk that ultimately gets priced into merchant fees. Always-on USD settlement is unglamorous plumbing, but it is precisely the kind of infrastructure that determines whether UPI corridors feel instant end-to-end or merely instant for the payer. It is also the direction of travel across the industry: payment institutions are bolting real-time rails onto always-on settlement, because the "speed" of a corridor is the speed of its slowest leg. For Indian travellers and merchants, the test stays the same regardless: whether the FX conversion and settlement gains ever reach the receipt — and that remains a disclosure problem more than a technology one.

## The pattern: two globalisations of one stack

Read together, the week's developments describe India running two globalisation strategies in parallel. UPI-outward — Zimbabwe's national backbone negotiations, sovereign builds in Namibia, Peru, Trinidad & Tobago, acceptance networks across 11 countries — sells India's rails as public infrastructure to the world. GIFT City-inward — Skydo's PSP licence, JioBlackRock's outbound fund — positions the same jurisdiction as the regulated gateway for Indian money moving out. Both strategies ultimately rest on the same consumer promise: that moving money across borders should be cheaper, faster and legible. Both also create the same class of risk: new intermediaries in new regulatory layers, each adding a fee or a failure mode that is invisible until something goes wrong. The verification habits that keep you safe are unglamorous and unchanged — check who is authorised to hold and move your money (RBI's PA-CB register for exporters, IFSCA's registry for GIFT City products), read the fee schedule including FX spreads, and treat announcement dates as talk-deadlines, not launch dates. October 31 is the date to watch in Harare; what matters is what actually goes live, and on whose terms.
