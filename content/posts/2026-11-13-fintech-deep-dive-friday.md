# Fintech Deep Dive — Friday | November 13, 2026

**Policy & Regulation | Developments: November 6 – November 13, 2026**

Last week's policy dive watched two regulators pull in one direction — RBI consolidating payment-security rules while SEBI opened gates for Razorpay and diaspora investors. This week the theme is repricing: three separate exercises in recalculating who carries risk and who captures margin. RBI codified the cash-remittance rail that India's internal migrants actually use, floated ownership curbs on the private equity firms assembling NBFC empires, and licensed a wallet into a lender. SEBI switched on a new settlement regime with fresh price tags for regulatory violations. And IRDAI's September proposal to cap insurance distribution costs claimed its first measured victim — a 49% drawdown in PB Fintech's stock, and a management admission that its agent model may not survive the final rules.

## 1. RBI consolidates the Domestic Money Transfer rulebook — and finally puts DMT customers under the ombudsman

Effective November 11, RBI issued the Domestic Money Transfer (DMT) Directions, 2026, folding years of scattered circulars into a single rulebook for cash-based remittances routed through banks and authorised payment operators ([RBI Master Directions](https://www.rbi.org.in/Scripts/BS_ViewMasDirections.aspx?id=13752); [Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/industry/banking/rbi-consolidates-money-transfer-rules-allows-50-000-cash-neft-transfers-126111101533_1.html)). The headline limits are continuity, not change: a walk-in customer with no bank account can remit up to ₹50,000 per transaction to a beneficiary's account via NEFT at a branch; through Business Correspondents and ATMs the cap is ₹5,000 per transaction and ₹25,000 a month per remitter; and cash pay-out to a beneficiary without an account is capped at ₹10,000 per transaction, ₹25,000 a month per beneficiary ([Hindu BusinessLine](https://www.thehindubusinessline.com/money-and-banking/banks-can-offer-cash-pay-in-and-pay-out-services-under-revised-domestic-money-transfer-norms/article71690997.ece); [Mint](https://www.livemint.com/money/personal-finance/no-bank-account-check-how-much-cash-you-can-send-or-receive-through-atm-neft-under-rbi-domestic-money-transfer-rules-11794442718991.html)). The plumbing around the caps is where the substance sits: remitters must register with a verified mobile number and a self-certified Officially Valid Document under the KYC Directions 2025, every transaction needs an additional factor of authentication, and cash-remittance messages must carry a marker identifier so receiving banks can monitor them separately — with velocity checks and mandatory investigation of unusual credit spikes ([Moneycontrol](https://www.moneycontrol.com/news/business/banks/rbi-caps-cash-transfers-under-domestic-money-transfer-services-at-rs-10-000-per-transaction-consolidates-dmt-rules-14077363.html)).

Two consumer-relevant points got less coverage than the ₹50,000 headline. First, banks and non-banks may charge for DMT services, and the Directions explicitly route complaints into the RBI Integrated Ombudsman Scheme, 2026 — the first time the consolidated rule spells out that a migrant worker whose ₹10,000 cash pay-out vanishes in transit has a defined escalation path, not just a customer-care black hole ([Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/industry/banking/rbi-consolidates-money-transfer-rules-allows-50-000-cash-neft-transfers-126111101533_1.html)). Second, the Directions bind DMT into the Income-tax Act 2025's cash-deposit provisions — the compliance perimeter around cash remittances is now statutory, not circular. This is the unglamorous rail of Indian fintech: the BPO worker in Gurugram sending wages to a village with no UPI acceptor, the family receiving cash at a BC agent because the nearest bank branch is 40 kilometres away. Consolidation makes the rules legible; the ombudsman line makes them enforceable. Both were overdue.

## 2. RBI weighs a one-NBFC-per-PE rule: ownership concentration becomes the new systemic worry

Reuters reported on November 12 that RBI is working on proposals to bar private equity firms from holding controlling stakes in more than one non-banking financial company, with stakes of 15% or less reclassified as passive financial investments facing lighter scrutiny; a draft would go through public consultation and could be formally issued early next year ([Reuters via Mint](https://www.livemint.com/industry/banking/india-plans-curbs-on-private-equity-ownership-of-nbfcs-report-11794472084478.html); [Times of India](https://timesofindia.indiatimes.com/business/india-business/rbi-plans-to-ban-private-equity-firms-from-holding-controlling-stakes-in-multiple-nbfcs-what-will-change/articleshow/135761245.cms)). The stated logic is contagion: if a global PE house hits distress, RBI does not want the shock propagating simultaneously through half a dozen NBFCs it controls — lenders that in aggregate supply much of India's gold-loan, vehicle-finance and microcredit book ([Freepress Journal](https://www.freepressjournal.in/business/rbi-plans-new-rules-to-restrict-private-equity-control-in-multiple-nbfcs-may-ease-scrutiny-on-stakes-up-to-15)). The current architecture already requires prior permission for acquisitions of 26% or more and borrows control definitions from SEBI's takeover code; February's conditional clearance of Bain-linked entities holding up to 41.66% in Manappuram Finance is the template case that likely sharpened the thinking ([streamlinefeed summary of the Reuters report](https://streamlinefeed.co.ke/news/rbi-reportedly-weighs-limits-on-private-equity-nbfc-control)).

For consumers this is abstract-sounding but material. NBFC ownership concentration is credit-supply concentration: when a handful of sponsors control many lenders, a single sponsor's funding crunch can synchronise credit tightening across gold loans, two-wheeler finance and microfinance at once — the segments where India's most rate-sensitive borrowers live. The industry counter is already on record: IVCA has asked RBI to permit group entities above 20% in multiple NBFCs, arguing portfolio diversification is the point of multi-platform investing ([streamlinefeed](https://streamlinefeed.co.ke/news/rbi-reportedly-weighs-limits-on-private-equity-nbfc-control)). The open questions that will decide whether this is consumer protection or capital gating: how the final rules define control for funds under one manager, what transition terms existing multi-NBFC portfolios get, and whether forced unwinds concentrate the market further rather than diversifying it. Until the consultation draft lands, treat every number here as a source-sourced proposal, not a rule.

## 3. MobiKwik becomes an NBFC: the licence is the business model now

On November 6, One MobiKwik Systems received RBI's final Certificate of Registration for its wholly owned subsidiary MobiKwik Financial Services to operate as a non-banking financial company — converting the April in-principle approval into the ability to originate loans on its own balance sheet rather than only referring users to partner lenders as an Lending Service Provider ([CNBC TV18 via TradingView](https://www.tradingview.com/news/cnbctv:c9e6a42fc094b:0); [scanX](https://scanx.trade/stock-market-news/companies/one-mobikwik-systems-gets-rbi-nod-nbfc-operations/55501801)). Management's stated plan: target ₹5,000 crore in loans, push lending to roughly half of revenue, and run a 10:90 co-lending structure where MobiKwik's underwriting meets bank capital ([CNBC TV18 via TradingView](https://www.tradingview.com/news/cnbctv:c9e6a42fc094b:0); [Sahi](https://www.sahi.com/news/one-mobikwik-systems-gets-rbi-approval-for-non-banking-financial-operations)). The company claims 196 million users and 5 million merchants to cross-sell into.

The regulatory reading matters more than the funding reading. India's digital lending rules deliberately made the referral-only LSP model thin-margin and compliance-heavy — first-loss guarantees capped, fee disclosure mandated, recovery conduct pinned on the regulated lender. The predictable consequence is verticalisation: platforms that can, become the regulated entity. MobiKwik follows the playbook other payment majors have run, and the August 2026 transfer of its digital lending business into a subsidiary with fresh equity was the obvious preparatory step ([Sahi](https://www.sahi.com/news/one-mobikwik-systems-gets-rbi-approval-for-non-banking-financial-operations)). For the consumer, the change is real: when the recharge app becomes your lender, the credit decision, the pricing and the recovery are all internal to one platform, and the app-store convenience that made borrowing one tap away also makes default path-dependent on the same closed loop. RBI's guardrails apply in full to NBFC digital lending — but a ₹5,000 crore target built on 196 million users is a live test of whether an RBACE-style, data-rich underwriting stack beats old-fashioned NIM discipline. Watch the NPA disclosure cadence, not the revenue mix, as the honest scorecard.

## 4. SEBI's new settlement regime goes live: faster exits, higher floor prices

The SEBI (Settlement of Administrative and Civil Proceedings) Regulations, 2026 — notified October 8 — took effect on November 7, replacing the 2018 regime with an online application facility, a settlement-amount calculator, FAQs and a help desk ([ETBFSI/IANS](https://bfsi.economictimes.indiatimes.com/articles/sebis-new-settlement-regulations-come-into-force-with-online-filing-fast-track-mechanism/135654078); [Elets BFSI](https://bfsi.eletsonline.com/sebi-settlement-online-rules)). The new price structure: application fees of ₹25,000 for individuals and ₹35,000 for other entities, minimum settlement amounts of ₹3 lakh for first-time settlers and ₹7 lakh otherwise, a 90-day window from the show-cause notice, and a transitional 90-day route for pending cases priced 20% higher — an explicit nudge to clear the legacy docket under the new book. A fast-track mechanism covers limited categories of matters ([Elets BFSI](https://bfsi.eletsonline.com/sebi-settlement-online-rules)).

For fintechs, this is the exit economics of enforcement. Most mid-sized compliance failures — disclosure lapses, KYC/AML process breaks, intermediary conduct issues — end in settlement rather than contested orders, so the floor prices and digital rails in these regulations shape what a regulatory mistake actually costs. The digitisation and the calculator are genuine access improvements; the higher minimums cut the other way for small registered intermediaries and first-offender individuals, for whom ₹3 lakh plus fees is no longer a rounding error. The 20% transitional surcharge is honest incentives — settle now, don't wait — but watch whether it produces rushed settlements of cases that deserved adjudication. As always with settlement: no admission of guilt, no precedent, and no public findings — efficiency for the docket, opacity for the market.

## 5. IRDAI's distribution caps meet PB Fintech's P&L: the POSP model on trial

The week's sharpest regulator-versus-business collision played out around IRDAI's September consultation paper, "Recalibrating Economics of Insurance Distribution," which proposes re-imposing expense-of-management caps — 12.5% of premiums for life insurers and 20% of gross direct premium income for general insurers over five years — on the argument that commission payouts have grown disproportionately since caps were scrapped in 2023 ([Medianama](https://www.medianama.com/2026/11/223-irdai-pb-fintech-non-insurance-services)). The paper has cost PB Fintech nearly half its market value: the stock closed earlier this week at ₹953.80, down about 49% from ₹1,886.30 before the consultation, and JM Financial cut its FY28 EBITDA estimates to reflect the proposed caps ([Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/markets/news/pb-fintech-retailers-buy-fpis-turn-wary-in-q2-who-got-right-end-of-deal-126111100075_1.html)). On the Q2 FY27 call, management was blunt: PB Partners — the offline agent network earning roughly ₹700 crore in the September quarter — runs a loss-making point-of-sale-person (POSP) business that the current regulatory structure "does not leave scope for... the way it is today," with the CEO having warned in September that the caps could erase up to 30% of revenue ([Medianama](https://www.medianama.com/2026/11/223-irdai-pb-fintech-non-insurance-services); [Trade Brains](https://tradebrains.in/premium/pb-fintech-shareholders-guide-what-the-q2-call-admitted-what-it-avoided-and-what-comes-next-12779925)).

Strip out the stock drama and this is a distribution-economics fight with a consumer stake at its centre. Intermediary commissions are embedded in every premium — capping them should, in principle, be pro-consumer. But the pass-through assumption is unverified: nothing in the proposal obliges insurers to return the saved commission to policyholders as lower premiums rather than absorbing it as margin. Meanwhile the sector PB Partners built — trained POSP agents selling simple products in tier-2/3 towns — is precisely the assisted-distribution layer that got first-time buyers into term and health cover. If the final rules kill the unit economics of that layer without a replacement, the consumer loses an access channel; if the caps hold and insurers pass savings through, consumers gain twice. The decisive variables to watch in the final framework: whether renewal commissions are grandfathered, what transition time is granted, and whether IRDAI couples the caps with any premium-reduction expectation. A 30% revenue-erasure number and a 49% stock drawdown are management's framing of the stakes — the regulator's counter-framing has not yet been published, and consumers should insist it is.

## The takeaway

No single directive dominated this week, and that is the story. Five separate repricings — of a cash rail (DMT), of ownership concentration (PE-NBFC), of platform economics (MobiKwik's licence), of enforcement cost (SEBI settlements), of distribution margin (IRDAI caps) — are each moving risk and reward between consumers, platforms, investors and the state. The pattern worth tracking into next week: consolidation measures make rules legible while raising their floor prices, and every floor price in this week's news ultimately lands on a retail balance sheet — as a fee, a rejected loan, a pricier settlement, or a premium.
