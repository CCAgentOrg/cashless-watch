# Fintech Deep Dive — Friday | November 20, 2026

*Policy & Regulation | Developments reported November 13–20, 2026*

This was a week in which India's financial regulators were mostly seen retreating, pruning and preparing. NPCI's merchant-fee framework shrank from a percentage-based levy to a flat ₹5 for business-to-business payments — and still has no launch date, with reports now suggesting the rollout may wait until after next year's assembly elections. The RBI fined a listed wallet company and scrubbed 49 NBFC licences off its register in a single Friday announcement. SEBI promised AI guardrails with named human accountability, even as it weighed relaxing the ownership look-through that asks foreign investors to say who they really are. And Razorpay, the payments giant whose IPO approval passed through SEBI's confidential filing route, prepared to publish its draft papers this week. Here is what each move means for the consumer.

## 1. UPI's merchant fee shrinks again: a flat ₹5 for business payments — and still no launch date

The week's dominant policy story was the continued shrinking of NPCI's UPI merchant discount rate (MDR) framework. On November 18, Business Standard reported that NPCI's UPI and Services Steering Committee has decided that merchant-to-merchant (M2M) transactions — effectively business-to-business payments — will attract a flat ₹5 charge, dramatically lower than the ₹80–300 that traders had feared under the original framework ([Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/industry/banking/flat-5-mdr-fee-likely-for-b2b-upi-payments-against-feared-300-charge-126111801534_1.html); [Mint](https://www.livemint.com/money/upi-fee-shock-eased-b2b-transactions-may-now-attract-just-5-charge-under-revised-mdr-plan-11795088050504.html)). The arithmetic explains the relief: under the 0.4% MDR announced in September for merchant payments above ₹2,000, a transaction of ₹20,000–40,000 would have cost ₹80–160, and anything at ₹75,000 or more would have hit the ₹300 cap ([Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/industry/banking/flat-5-mdr-fee-likely-for-b2b-upi-payments-against-feared-300-charge-126111801534_1.html)). The decision came days after the All India Consumer Products Distributors Federation wrote to Finance Minister Nirmala Sitharaman seeking a full M2M exemption from the framework ([Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/industry/banking/flat-5-mdr-fee-likely-for-b2b-upi-payments-against-feared-300-charge-126111801534_1.html)).

The consumer-facing parts of the framework remain untouched by this week's revision. More than 95% of person-to-merchant transactions stay outside MDR's scope, small merchants under the P2PM model keep zero MDR, and the flat ₹5 on select categories — railways, telecom, insurance, fuel — above ₹2,000 survives unchanged, covering roughly 4% of merchant transactions in total ([Mint](https://www.livemint.com/money/upi-fee-shock-eased-b2b-transactions-may-now-attract-just-5-charge-under-revised-mdr-plan-11795088050504.html); [CNBC-TV18](https://www.cnbctv18.com/business/b2b-upi-payments-may-see-flat-5-mdr-rollout-date-still-unclear-20037392.htm)). What changed this week is the timeline: the rollout, originally scheduled for October 15 and postponed after retailer objections, now has no decided date at all. The Financial Express reported on November 19 that implementation may be delayed beyond the 2027 assembly elections in five states, as the government weighs merchant backlash against timing concerns ([Financial Express](https://www.facebook.com/thefinancialexpress/posts/the-implementation-of-merchant-discount-rate-mdr-on-select-upi-transactions-may-/1614850224003897)). Markets priced in the drift immediately: Paytm and Pine Labs shares plunged on November 19 as the delay sank in — a stark reminder that "free UPI," six years running, is also an industry model that cannot monetise its core volume ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/world/india/paytm-pine-labs-shares-plunge-upi-transaction-fee-rollout-india-delayed-2026-11-19)).

For consumers, two things are worth holding onto. First, the original sin of this framework is process, not substance: a 0.4% levy on large merchant payments is modest by global standards, but it was announced in September, deferred in October under retailer pressure, and re-scoped in November — three structural revisions in two months, each discovered by traders through press leaks rather than a public consultation document with a comment window. Payment-policy by steering committee, revised on the march, is exactly how consumer trust erodes even when the final charge lands at ₹5. Second, watch what happens to the proceeds. The stated rationale for MDR is to fund the ecosystem — banks, PSPs, and fraud-loss absorption — so that zero-MDR consumer UPI stays free. If the flat-₹5 compromise passes but interchange economics stay opaque, consumers will have gained a fee and lost the funding story that justified it.

## 2. RBI's enforcement week: a ₹9 lakh fine for Mobikwik and 49 NBFC licences gone

The RBI's supervisory apparatus produced two public enforcement actions this week, both disclosed on November 13 and 16 respectively — and both reward a closer read than their headline sizes suggest.

The first was a ₹9 lakh penalty on One Mobikwik Systems, imposed by order dated November 6 and disclosed on November 13, for non-compliance with RBI directions on prepaid payment instruments and fraud reporting ([RBI press release](https://rbi.org.in/Scripts/BS_PressReleaseDisplay.aspx?prid=63973); [Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/finance/news/rbi-fines-mobikwik-9-lakh-over-ppi-transfer-and-fraud-reporting-lapses-126111301793_1.html)). The two named violations deserve attention: the wallet allowed fund transfers by PPI holders to non-pre-registered beneficiaries beyond prescribed limits, and it failed to report certain frauds within the prescribed timeline ([Economic Times](https://m.economictimes.com/news/economy/policy/rbi-imposes-monetary-penalty-on-one-mobikwik-systems/articleshow/135809404.cms)). The underlying inspection covered June 2025 to March 2026. The consumer significance is not the ₹9 lakh — that is a parking fine for a listed fintech — but the specific failure: delayed fraud reporting is the difference between a frozen fraudulent transfer and a vanished one. Every wallet user's protection against a drained balance runs through the clock that starts when the company notices fraud, and a wallet that files those reports late has quietly extended the thief's window. Intermediary-level fraud reporting to the RBI feeds the fraud registry ecosystem consumers depend on; lapses there degrade protection for everyone, not just Mobikwik's own users.

The second action was structural. On November 16, the RBI cancelled the Certificates of Registration of 49 NBFCs under Section 45-IA(6) of the RBI Act — with the cancellation orders issued between October 1 and 29 — while separately noting that 13 NBFCs and one housing finance company surrendered their registrations, bringing 63 entities off the register in one announcement ([CNBC-TV18](https://www.cnbctv18.com/business/finance/rbi-cancels-registrations-of-49-nbfcs-13-nbfcs-one-hfc-surrender-licences-20035213.htm); [Economic Times](https://m.economictimes.com/industry/banking/finance/rbi-cancels-registrations-of-49-nbfcs-13-nbfcs-and-one-hfc-surrender-licenses/articleshow/135882181.cms)). The geography is the story within the story: 43 of the 49 cancelled NBFCs had registered offices in West Bengal, 41 in Kolkata alone, and nine shared a single address at Poddar Court on Rabindra Sarani ([ETBFSI](https://bfsi.economictimes.indiatimes.com/articles/rbi-cancels-registrations-of-43-west-bengal-nbfcs-41-in-kolkata-nine-share-one-address/135900686)). The RBI has not disclosed individual ownership or grounds for each cancellation, but the concentration pattern fits the known triggers — failure to meet the ₹10 crore net-owned-fund requirement under the scale-based regulation glide path (deadline March 2027), failure to file returns, or simply not carrying on NBFC business at all. The cleanup arrives as the RBI also prepares tighter rules on private equity ownership of NBFCs ([Business Today](https://www.businesstoday.in/latest/corporate/story/rbi-cancels-registration-of-49-nbfcs-amid-plans-to-tighten-private-equity-ownership-rules-567726-2026-11-16)). For consumers, a smaller, better-capitalised NBFC register is unambiguously good: shell NBFCs are the natural habitat of co-lending structures and lending-app supply chains that have repeatedly surfaced in digital-lending complaints. The open question is whether the register's health is matched by complaint-handling capacity at the survivors — consolidation without conduct reform just concentrates the harm.

## 3. SEBI readies AI guardrails for capital markets — with a human in the loop

SEBI Chairman Tuhin Kanta Pandey confirmed this week that the regulator will issue detailed guidelines for responsible AI use in capital markets, built on three pillars: disclosure of AI use, accountability, and a mandatory human-in-the-loop approach to protect investors ([Elets BFSI](https://bfsi.eletsonline.com/sebi-to-issue-ai-guardrails-for-capital-markets-with-human-oversight)). The framework builds on SEBI's June 2025 consultation paper on responsible use of AI and machine learning in securities markets, and industry reporting indicates final rules are expected around November 2026 ([Elets BFSI](https://bfsi.eletsonline.com/sebi-to-issue-ai-guardrails-for-capital-markets-with-human-oversight)). This lands at a moment when AI has quietly become infrastructure across the retail-investing experience — robo-advisory feeds, AI-scored loan offers inside broking apps, algorithmic execution, chatbots answering suitability questions — largely without any named human being accountable when the model errs.

The human-in-the-loop mandate is the consumer-relevant core. Regulated entities will not be able to disclaim an AI-driven mis-selling event as "the model's output"; a designated human remains on the hook. That converts a diffuse technical risk into an assignable legal one, which is precisely what the consumer protection framework in securities law has always required of human advisors. The open questions are enforcement-shaped: will disclosures of AI use reach investors in plain language at the point of recommendation, or sit in policy documents nobody reads? Will back-testing and model-change records be auditable by SEBI in real time, or produced only after harm? And will the guardrails cover the marketing layer — AI-generated investment content from unregistered influencers — which sits outside the regulated entity entirely and is where most retail investors actually encounter AI advice today? SEBI has moved aggressively on algorithmic manipulation in the past; extending that instinct to the AI layer, including the unregistered content layer, is the test of whether these guardrails are more than a compliance memo.

## 4. SEBI may relax the FPI ownership look-through to 25% — transparency in reverse?

While promising AI accountability, SEBI is reportedly considering loosening a transparency requirement. According to Fortune India, the regulator may propose raising the beneficial-ownership identification threshold for foreign portfolio investors to 25%, benchmarking against developed and emerging jurisdictions that use the same figure, with a consultation paper planned ([Fortune India](https://www.fortuneindia.com/markets/sebi-may-propose-raising-fpi-beneficial-ownership-threshold-to-25-plans-consultation-paper/170518)). An official noted that changes to certain Prevention of Money Laundering Act provisions and rules would require government intervention, while SEBI could act on other measures through its own consultation process ([Fortune India](https://www.fortuneindia.com/markets/sebi-may-propose-raising-fpi-beneficial-ownership-threshold-to-25-plans-consultation-paper/170518)).

The tension is worth naming plainly. After the 2023–24 short-seller episode exposed how little was known about who stood behind certain India-focused FPI structures, SEBI tightened granular disclosure requirements — a consumer-protection measure in the broadest sense, since ultimate-ownership opacity is where concentrated, coordinated market positions hide. Raising the look-through threshold to 25% reduces the disclosure burden on compliant FPIs and aligns with FATF-style international norms, but it also widens the space in which layered ownership can pool below the reporting line. India's capital-market regulators have historically justified stricter-than-global standards on the ground that domestic retail flows into our markets deserve more protection than the global baseline. If the easing proceeds, the test should be whether SEBI simultaneously strengthens its ability to demand look-through when concentration patterns emerge — a targeted-piercing power rather than a blanket exemption. Retail investors are the residual claimants on market integrity; the consultation paper, when it arrives, deserves comment letters asking exactly that.

## 5. Razorpay's SEBI-cleared IPO heads for a public filing — the first real test of payments economics

Razorpay, the Tiger Global-backed payments unicorn, received SEBI's observations on its confidentially-filed IPO papers on November 3 and is preparing to publicly file its draft red herring prospectus as early as this week, with the offering — up to $600 million (₹5,000–6,000 crore) through fresh shares and an offer-for-sale — targeting a valuation around $5 billion and a listing that could come as early as February 2027 ([Kotak Neo](https://www.kotakneo.com/news/ipos/razorpay-ipo-everything-investors-need-to-know); [Indian Startup News/Bloomberg](https://www.facebook.com/indianstartupnews/posts/tiger-global-backed-fintech-unicorn-razorpay-is-reportedly-preparing-to-publicly/1795638742195683)). Note the valuation's direction: $5 billion is below the $7.5 billion the company commanded in its 2021 private round — public-market discipline arriving at the door of India's largest payment processors ([Kotak Neo](https://www.kotakneo.com/news/ipos/razorpay-ipo-everything-investors-need-to-know)). The company completed its redomiciliation from the US to India in May 2025 specifically to enable this listing ([Kotak Neo](https://www.kotakneo.com/news/ipos/razorpay-ipo-everything-investors-need-to-know)).

Two policy angles make this a regulation story rather than a funding story. First, the route: Razorpay used SEBI's confidential pre-filing framework — draft papers kept private until observations are received — which is designed to let issuers test regulatory appetite without leaking strategy to competitors. It worked as intended, but it also means the market will see the risk factors, the regulator's concerns implicitly baked in, only at the public filing stage. Investors should read those risk factors with the week's other stories in mind: the MDR delay is a direct regulatory-risk line item for a company whose processing volumes ride on UPI's zero-MDR economics, and the RBI's fraud-reporting enforcement against Mobikwik previews the compliance exposure of every PPA/PSP in the country. Second, the listing is the first major public test of whether India's payment-infrastructure layer can deliver returns to public shareholders under a policy regime where the state explicitly holds the consumer-facing price at zero. India has raised approximately $18.6 billion through IPOs in 2026 so far ([Indian Startup News/Bloomberg](https://www.facebook.com/indianstartupnews/posts/tiger-global-backed-fintech-unicorn-razorpay-is-reportedly-preparing-to-publicly/1795638742195683)); Razorpay's pricing will tell us how the market values regulated infrastructure whose unit economics are set in NPCI committee rooms. For consumers, public listing brings quarterly disclosure of complaint volumes, fraud numbers and regulatory dependencies — a transparency upgrade the private markets never demanded.

## Also on the radar

- **FEMA relief for freelancers**: Mint's explainer this week walked through the RBI's second amendment to the FEMA export-import regulations (dated November 2, effective October 1): resident individuals are now deemed to have filed the Export Declaration Form once payment details reach their bank, regardless of amount, and other exporters get the same for service remittances up to ₹10 lakh — plus export realisation windows shortened from 15 months to 9 ([Mint](https://www.livemint.com/money/personal-finance/rbi-edf-rules-freelancers-service-export-reporting/amp-11795066137821.html); [Unnathi Partners](https://unnathipartners.com/fema-export-import-regulations-2026)). Real paperwork relief for India's gig-export economy; banks retain the IDPMS reporting burden above the threshold.
- **DPDP countdown pressure on fintech vendors**: With the DPDP Act's main duties starting May 2027, lenders are preparing to demand breach-notification timelines (Rule 7: board and customer notice without delay, full board report within 72 hours) and security-safeguard contracts from their fintech processors under Section 8(2) — meaning fintechs without documented security practices will face contract-level exclusion before any regulator acts ([The Fintech Times](https://thefintechtimes.com/what-indian-lenders-will-ask-their-fintech-partners-under-dpdp)).
- **The compliance cautionary tale**: Chennai-based fintech Bulkpe reported a cyber theft of roughly ₹1 crore on November 17, with unauthorised transfers executed within a single hour — a small-scale illustration of why the RBI's cyber-resilience master directions for payment operators, and the fraud-reporting clock it enforces, exist ([Money Matters Club/Bulkpe reports](https://www.facebook.com/seshadri.baskar.3/posts/hackers-recently-targeted-bulkpe-a-chennai-based-fintech-company-stealing-approx/40482201804759822)).
