# Fintech Weekly Deep Dive — The Fee That Exists Everywhere Except Checkout | Week of November 16–22, 2026

## Executive Summary

India's payments industry spent this week negotiating with a fee that will not launch. The merchant discount rate (MDR) on select UPI person-to-merchant transactions — 0.4% above ₹2,000, capped at ₹300 — was announced in September with an October 15 start, deferred on October 13 to "immediately post Diwali," and now, per a Financial Express report on November 19, may wait until after the 2027 assembly elections in Uttar Pradesh, Punjab, Uttarakhand, Goa and Manipur. The government, RBI and NPCI have completed every piece of groundwork except the one thing that matters: a date.

Markets repriced the vacuum instantly. On November 19, One 97 Communications (Paytm) fell as much as 9.1% intraday, Mobikwik dropped 8.7% and Pine Labs slid 5.5%, as investors digested Jefferies' estimate that UPI MDR would account for roughly 14% of Paytm's earnings — and 28% of its FY28 profit before tax in a full-year scenario. The Payments Council of India has made multiple representations to the finance ministry asking, in effect, to be told when it will be allowed to earn money. Meanwhile NPCI has already notified a scheme that spends the fee's proceeds — ₹300 per QR code and ₹750 per soundbox for small merchants, funded from MDR collections — for a levy that does not yet exist.

The week's takeaway is uncomfortable: UPI's merchant fee has become a policy that exists everywhere except at checkout — in statute, in NPCI's circulars, in analyst spreadsheets, in stock prices — but not in force. That is a story about process failure (four structural revisions in two months, each discovered by press leak rather than consultation), about a monetisation failure (an industry whose core product legally cannot be priced), and above all about political economy: six years after Parliament froze merchant fees at zero, the calendar for unfreezing them is being set by an election schedule, not by a payment system's economics. For consumers, nothing changes at checkout today — and the protections that matter (P2P stays free, ~96% of merchant transactions stay outside the fee, pass-through to customers is prohibited) survived every revision this week. What eroded instead is trust in how payment policy gets made.

## The Story in Depth

### Context: the six-year zero-MDR experiment, and why ending it needed Parliament

To understand why a 0.4% fee can paralyse a market, start with how strange India's starting position was. From UPI's 2016 launch until December 2019, UPI person-to-merchant transactions carried an MDR of up to 0.30%, like any card rail. On January 1, 2020, that ended not by pricing decision but by statute: Section 10A of the Payment and Settlement Systems Act, 2007 (inserted by the Finance (No. 2) Act, 2019) prohibited any MDR on UPI and RuPay debit transactions, backed by Section 269SU of the Income-tax Act, which compelled large businesses to offer these modes. No other large payments market had written "free" into law; most countries treat merchant fees as a matter of market negotiation or regulatory guidance. India made changing its mind a parliamentary act.

The state then paid for the free lunch it ordered. A government incentive scheme reimbursed banks and payment providers — ₹1,389 crore in FY22, ₹2,210 crore in FY23, ₹3,631 crore in FY24, cumulatively ₹8,276 crore through FY25. It worked as industrial policy: UPI became the world's largest real-time payment system, this October crossing ₹31.53 lakh crore in a single month, and DFS's own impact study found 94% of surveyed small merchants had adopted UPI, with QR deployments growing from 9.3 crore to roughly 65.8 crore.

But the subsidy was always designed to end. The Department of Financial Services told a parliamentary panel it was examining restoring MDR on high-value transactions alongside a tiered structure to "phase out government support in the next few years." The incentives in fact collapsed before the fee arrived: disbursements fell to ₹1,046 crore in FY25, no fresh payout has been made since April 2025, and the FY27 budget carries just ₹2,000 crore against FY24's ₹3,631 crore actual. The state has already stopped paying; the market has not yet been allowed to start.

The legal gate opened this August. Parliament amended Section 10A (Taxation and Other Laws (Amendment) Act 2026, presidential assent August 17), the Lok Sabha passing it on August 6, so that zero-MDR protection now covers only transactions at or below ₹2,000. On September 14, the finance ministry notified S.O. 5067(E); on September 15, NPCI's circular and FAQ crystallised the framework — 0.4% on eligible P2M payments above ₹2,000, capped at ₹300 for transactions of ₹75,000 and above, effective October 15. The ministry simultaneously clarified the consumer protections: P2P transfers stay free at any amount (roughly 70% of transaction value sits outside the framework), about 96% of merchant transactions remain free, and small merchants on the P2PM model stay exempt.

The counter-mobilisation was immediate and politically potent. On September 30, a delegation led by CAIT secretary general Praveen Khandelwal — a BJP MP — and AIMRA's Kailash Lakhyani met Finance Minister Nirmala Sitharaman; a planned "No UPI Day" strike on October 2 was called off, with the traders extracting a hearing and submitting asks: phase the fee in at 0.20% in FY27, rise 0.05 percentage points annually, and lift the ₹1 lakh monthly small-merchant threshold to ₹5 lakh. On October 13, NPCI's UPI and Services Steering Committee deferred the levy "immediately post Diwali at a date to be notified separately." It has not been notified since.

### What Happened This Week: four beats in five days

**Monday's groundwork continued even as the date didn't.** NPCI's November 11 Vyapaar Saksham circular, analysed in Sunday's deep dive, does more than promise ₹300 per QR code and ₹750 per soundbox for Tier 3–6 towns, the North-East, J&K and Ladakh; its Annexure II mandates merchant geocode reporting through the RespAuth API, so every incentive claim will be validated against transaction-level coordinates. The plumbing for a fee-funded world is being finished while the fee itself idles — the scheme runs three years from the date MDR is implemented, a date that does not exist.

**Wednesday, the industry asked for a date.** The Payments Council of India made multiple representations to the finance ministry and NPCI seeking early announcement of the final implementation date, flagging that government incentives for processing UPI and RuPay debit transactions have been outstanding for over 18 months while technology, acquisition and compliance costs keep rising. NPCI chief executive Dilip Asbe has put the ecosystem's annual operating cost at around ₹21,000 crore; the levy, when it comes, is expected to recover ₹13,000–15,000 crore in year one. The same day, Business Standard reported the framework's second retreat: merchant-to-merchant (effectively B2B) payments, which traders feared would attract ₹80–300 under the 0.4% structure, will instead carry a flat ₹5 — a decision that came days after the All India Consumer Products Distributors Federation wrote to the finance minister seeking a full M2M exemption.

**Thursday, the election timetable surfaced and the market crashed.** The Financial Express reported that implementation may slip past the 2027 assembly elections in five states — Uttar Pradesh, Punjab, Uttarakhand, Goa and Manipur — as the government weighs merchant backlash, with one senior industry official calling a delay beyond the Uttar Pradesh polls a "worst-case scenario" and adding, "I think it will get implemented. I think it's only a timing issue." Traders read the sentence "only a timing issue" and sold: Paytm fell as much as 9.1% intraday to ₹1,715 (closing 8.7% lower), Mobikwik 8.7% to ₹206.80, and Pine Labs 5.5% to ₹159.90. Jefferies estimated the fees would have accounted for roughly 14% of Paytm's earnings; its base case had MDR at 8–12% of FY27 EPS with a fourth-quarter rollout and 28% of FY28 profit before tax in a full-year scenario, warning the stock "may see overhang till clarity comes." Bernstein held its Buy at a ₹2,200 target, calling the delay "the most immediate risk" to near-term earnings.

**Friday and Saturday, the scaffolding around the gap grew taller.** Company-level arithmetic resurfaced: Pine Labs CEO Amrish Rau has told analysts MDR could unlock ₹80–230 crore of annual revenue, with a commitment to deploy roughly 10 lakh soundboxes; Paytm has pegged its own windfall near ₹700 crore, with analysts running figures as high as ₹1,160 crore. And the Supreme Court angle sharpened: the zero-MDR challenge proceeds without a stay, NPCI's counter-affidavit rests on the argument that ~96% of merchant payments are unaffected, and the bench in the digital-arrest suo motu case — which has also sought RBI's list of authorised institutions — hears the matter on November 23. NPCI has told the court that the prohibition on passing MDR to customers is a "mandatory and binding direction" enforceable up to termination.

### Why It Matters: three failures, one calendar

**The process failure.** The framework has now been revised four times in ten weeks — announced September 15 at 0.4%, deferred October 13, re-scoped November 18 to a flat ₹5 for B2B, and timeline-slipped November 19 toward post-election territory — and every revision reached traders and markets through press leaks, not a published consultation paper with a comment window. The irony is sharp in a week when SEBI was publishing consultation papers on FPI disclosure and exchange governance with dated comment deadlines. Payment-policy-by-steering-committee is precisely how consumer and merchant trust erodes even when the final charge lands at ₹5. A fee is a price; a price discovered by rumor is a tax on confidence.

**The monetisation failure.** Zero-MDR was a policy choice, but the industry's near-total dependence on its reversal was a business-model choice — and this week exposed it. Paytm, the country's flagship consumer payments company, has built a valuation in which more than a quarter of next-year profitability rests on a date that three institutions (government, RBI, NPCI) have failed to agree on for over a month. Pine Labs' entire thesis is soundbox economics that switch on only when MDR does. When the core product of a listed industry cannot legally be priced, the industry's earnings do not live in product or distribution; they live in committee rooms. That is an unusual and fragile place for earnings to live, and equity markets now price Indian payments off political news flow rather than operational metrics.

**The political economy.** The deepest fact in this story is that zero-MDR was created by statute, and the merchants who benefit from it are a constituency no government approaches lightly — 65 crore QR codes deep, concentrated in exactly the small-town India that every party contests. CAIT's proximity to the ruling party (its secretary general sits in the Lok Sabha as a BJP MP) gave the traders' phase-down asks a hearing within days; the AICPDF's M2M exemption letter produced the flat-₹5 concession within a week. The result: a technical pricing decision has been converted into an electoral one. The five state polls of early-to-mid 2027 — with Uttar Pradesh, the largest prize, last — now function as the real regulatory calendar. An industry official can call this "only a timing issue," but a payment system whose monetisation date is an election schedule has outsourced its economics to politics, and everyone in the market now knows it.

**The subsidy gap is the quiet emergency.** While the fee debate consumes attention, the actual funding position has deteriorated: government incentive disbursements stopped after April 2025, FY27's budgeted ₹2,000 crore is less than a third of FY24's payout, and NPCI's Vyapaar Saksham scheme is contractually funded from MDR collections that do not yet exist. The ecosystem is being asked to bridge a funding gap with revenue from a launch that keeps not happening. If the delay extends deep into 2027, expect the pressure to surface in bank-level UPI investment decisions, fraud-loss absorption capacity, and — the consumer-visible version — the technical decline rates that already ran at 0.24% among top-20 remitter banks in September, which at 25 billion transactions a month means tens of millions of stuck payments.

## Consumer Impact

**Nothing changes at your checkout today, and most of it never will.** Person-to-person transfers stay free at any amount, transactions up to ₹2,000 stay protected by statute as amended, small merchants on the P2PM model stay exempt, and roughly 96% of merchant payments sit outside the fee. When the levy does go live, it is legally the merchant's cost: NPCI has told the Supreme Court that passing it to customers is prohibited by a binding direction. If any shop adds a "UPI charges" line to a bill above ₹2,000, that is the business's own choice, not a rule — and the complaint route is the RBI Integrated Ombudsman.

**Watch three things instead.** First, the fee's justification is ecosystem funding — soundboxes, fraud-loss absorption, reliability — so its consumer dividend should show up as fewer stuck payments; the current 0.24% technical decline rate among top remitter banks is the baseline to hold it against. Second, the merchant fee already exists on one rail: RuPay credit cards on UPI have carried interchange above ₹2,000 since 2022 — so if a merchant declines UPI for a big-ticket payment, check which instrument was behind the QR. Third, the trust cost of this week is real: when a fee's structure is revised by press leak four times in ten weeks, the protection to verify is the primary documents themselves — the PIB clarification of September 15 and NPCI's FAQ, not WhatsApp forwards about "UPI charges from tomorrow."

**For merchants and small businesses**: until notification, zero-MDR rules remain in force and no acquirer may bill you the new fee. If you are above the thresholds, use the delay to test what a 0.4% (or flat-₹5 B2B) cost would do to margins, and to make the trader bodies' case — the flat-₹5 concession proves the lobbying channel works.

## Looking Ahead

**November 23**: the Supreme Court hears the digital-arrest suo motu matter, where NPCI's authorised-institutions list and the zero-MDR challenge converge — the closest thing this framework has to a judicial deadline. **The Union Budget (February 1)**: watch whether the FY27 incentive line of ₹2,000 crore is defended, quietly lapsed, or converted into an MDR-transition bridge; a Budget that assumes MDR revenue is the strongest signal yet of a firm date. **Early-to-mid 2027 polls** in Punjab, Uttarakhand, Goa, Manipur and finally Uttar Pradesh: the worst-case implementation window now runs behind them, meaning a realistic go-live between February and May 2027 — or a full-year slip that forces the industry back to the government for bridge funding. **Vyapaar Saksham's clock**: its three-year life only begins when MDR does; each month of delay shrinks the acceptance-infrastructure window it was meant to fund. **PCI's patience**: the council has asked twice for a date; a third ask without an answer would signal a breakdown worth its own coverage. And on the consumer side, watch whether any acquirer tests billing infrastructure early — the first merchant billed before notification is the story to file.

The fee exists in statute, in NPCI's schemes, in analyst models and in stock charts. The only place it does not exist is at checkout. Until a date is notified, that gap — not the 0.4% — is the story of Indian payments.
