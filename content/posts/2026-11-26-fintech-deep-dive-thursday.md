# Fintech Deep Dive — Thursday | November 26, 2026

*International & Cross-Border (Global fintech, UPI abroad) | Developments reported November 19–26, 2026*

This was the week India's payment diplomacy had its trade-fair moment. At the Singapore FinTech Festival (November 18–20), NPCI International launched a whitepaper with the Asian Development Bank and EY-Parthenon that reads as a manifesto for exporting India's payments model, met Indonesian scheme bodies to push the long-gestating UPI–QRIS linkage, and then UPI touched a grocery trolley — going live across LuLu's UAE hypermarket chain with a 10% discount for Indian shoppers. Paytm, meanwhile, chose a different export route: buying a minority stake in a Southeast Asian payments firm instead of waiting for governments to connect rails. And the week's most uncomfortable data point arrived from a Thunes–Juniper Research index showing that India's own cross-border payments are the slowest and costliest in the region even as its rail gets applause abroad. Here is what each development means — for the companies, and for the people actually moving money across borders.

## 1. UPI moves from the tourist counter to the shopping aisle in the UAE

On November 24–25, NPCI International and LuLu Retail unveiled UPI payments across LuLu stores in the UAE, launched in the presence of India's Consul General in Dubai, with a 10% instant discount (capped at AED 30 — roughly ₹730) for Indian travellers paying via UPI ([NPCI International](https://www.instagram.com/p/DgJ_LJtu_ZA); [India in Dubai](https://x.com/cgidubai/status/2125284956335382924)). LuLu is the Gulf's largest pan-GCC retail chain, which makes this the biggest everyday-retail commitment UPI has landed abroad. It follows the go-live announced October 30 of UPI for online bookings at "At the Top, Burj Khalifa" — the first UAE attraction to accept UPI e-commerce, via NIPL's partnerships with NEOPAY and Emaar Entertainment — and at Resorts World Sentosa in Singapore with FOMO Pay, covering Universal Studios Singapore, the Singapore Oceanarium and Adventure Cove; Singapore welcomed 1.2 million Indian visitors in 2025 ([PIB](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2330558)). UPI is now operational in 11 countries — UAE, Singapore, France, Bhutan, Sri Lanka, Nepal, Mauritius, Qatar, Cambodia, Greece and the Maldives — with Uzbekistan signed up via a commercial agreement announced during the Prime Minister's visit on August 30, 2026 ([PIB](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2302657); [DD News](https://ddnews.gov.in/en/upi-enabled-for-online-bookings-at-burj-khalifa-resorts-world-sentosa)).

The pattern is deliberate: land UPI where Indian footfall concentrates, then climb the value chain from ticketed attractions to weekly grocery runs. The discount is the tell. Acceptance abroad is not free — the merchant side pays acquirer and scheme economics that are undisclosed in every one of this week's announcements — so NIPL and LuLu are effectively subsidising habit formation, one capped discount at a time. For the consumer, the value proposition is real and immediate: paying from an Indian account in UPI avoids the roughly 3% foreign-transaction markup most Indian cards add, and the familiar QR flow removes fumbling with a forex card at checkout. But read the fine print of the habit being built: at home, zero MDR for UPI was a policy decision that shifted merchant costs onto the system; abroad, the merchant discount rate is negotiated privately, and when the discount campaign ends, nothing obliges LuLu's UAE counters to stay free for anyone. The consumer protection that took a decade to build domestically does not travel with the QR code.

## 2. SFF 2026: NIPL publishes the playbook — "you don't need to copy UPI"

The Singapore FinTech Festival (November 18–20) doubled as India's payments trade show. The India Pavilion — presented by ITPO under the theme "Bharat SETU: A Bridge to the Future" — was inaugurated by Minister of State Jayant Chaudhary, visited by Singapore's ministers, and featured SEBI showcasing its tech-led market supervision ([INDiplomacy](https://indiplomacy.com/2026/11/23/india-pavilion-at-sff-2026-showcases-digital-innovation-and-fintech-opportunities)). The substantive launch came from NPCI International on November 20: "Payments Beyond Borders: Trusted Connections Across Geopolitical Boundaries", a whitepaper developed with the Asian Development Bank and EY-Parthenon ([NPCI International](https://x.com/NPCI_Intl/status/2123697622246715741)). Per coverage of the launch presentation, its central message is a partial retreat from the "clone UPI" framing: countries building instant-payment systems need open APIs, interoperable network linkages, common QR standards and trusted governance — not a wholesale import of India's stack ([Nocapvc](https://www.instagram.com/p/DgEUfpRmuNV)). NIPL also used the festival to meet Indonesia's RINTIS and ASPI, "aligning on accelerating the operationalisation of the UPI–QRIS linkage" ([NPCI International](https://x.com/NPCI_Intl/status/2123702933053493647)), and CEO Ritesh Shukla followed up with a November 23 article in Central Bank Payments News arguing that connected domestic networks are the future of cross-border payments ([NPCI International](https://www.facebook.com/npciinternational/posts/as-digital-economies-become-increasingly-interconnected-interoperable-payment-ne/122225371280431977)).

Two things deserve scrutiny. First, the doctrine shift is smart but unproven: bilateral city-pair linkages like UPI–PayNow (live since February 2024) remain the only working proof, and the Indonesia linkage is still being "accelerated" years after the countries agreed to pursue it — the distance between an MoU and a tourist tapping a QR in Bali is measured in years of central-bank rulemaking, not whitepapers. Second, the phrase "across geopolitical boundaries" in the title is doing real work. Payments interoperability has become an instrument of foreign policy — an alternative orbit to the card networks and dollar-correspondent banking that dominate cross-border flows — and India is openly positioning UPI as the reference architecture for countries that want sovereign rails without picking a Western or Chinese bloc. Uzbekistan's August agreement and the FINNEXT Asia forum in Tashkent on November 19, where Central Asian schemes discussed connecting national payment systems with NIPL's help, show the pitch landing in new geographies ([Times of Central Asia](https://timesca.com/finnext-asia-forum-central-asias-fintech-sector-turns-to-cross-border-payments-and-ai-as-investors-focus-on-returns)). For consumers, temper expectations: a whitepaper is not a wallet, and announcement velocity will keep outrunning corridor availability for years.

## 3. The uncomfortable mirror: India's own cross-border payments are the region's slowest and costliest

While UPI collected applause at SFF, the week's sharpest data cut the other way. Coverage on November 23 of the Thunes–Juniper Research "2026 Payments Interoperability Index" (surveying 6,700 consumers across ten countries, conducted April 2026; index published October) found Singapore leads APAC — and India anchors the wrong end: 54% of Indians sending international payments pay fees above 3%, above the UN's 3% sustainable-development target for remittance costs, and 46% of those receiving international payments in India typically wait over three days — the slowest in the comparison ([Fintech News Singapore](https://fintechnews.sg/141953/payments/what-were-the-top-cross-border-payment-trends-in-apac-in-2026)). India also posts the region's highest reported stablecoin use — 23% of respondents, ahead of China's 18% — yet actual stablecoin payment usage stays under 3%, with scam risk, not regulation, named the top barrier. Mobile wallets are already the default channel: 66% of Indian respondents receive international payments into a wallet.

The paradox resolves once you separate two Indias. The UPI being exported is a merchant-acceptance scheme for Indian travellers — it does nothing for the migrant worker in Dubai sending money home, the freelancer invoicing a US client, or the family receiving remittances into a bank account, all of whom still traverse the correspondent-banking swamp the index measures. India is the world's largest remittance recipient, so "slowest and costliest" is not a rounding error; it is a mass consumer-protection problem hiding inside a success story. The reform machinery is grinding, if slowly: RBI's new Foreign Exchange Management (Export and Import of Goods and Services) Regulations, 2026 took effect October 1, and amendments notified November 3 exempt resident individuals from declaration requirements on small-value service transactions and set a ₹10 lakh reporting threshold — real paperwork relief for small exporters and freelancers, though it simplifies process, not price ([Economic Times](https://www.facebook.com/EconomicTimes/posts/small-value-service-exports-and-imports-get-easier-forex-rules-the-rbi-has-notif/1639442264878342); [Exim SEO](https://exportimport.guru/Exim_SEO/Single/Exporting/rbi-new-edf-rules-2026-service-goods-exporters-india)). Razorpay's final IFSCA Payment Service Provider licence for GIFT City, announced November 3 after April's in-principle approval, lets it move money in and out of India under one regulatory framework — infrastructure aimed at exactly these flows ([Razorpay](https://razorpay.com/newsroom/razorpay-receives-final-ifsca-licence-for-gift-city-to-power-cross-border-money-movement); [Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/companies/news/razorpay-gets-final-ifsca-licence-cross-border-payments-gift-city-126110200734_1.html)). The demand side is visible in RBI's LRS data too: FY26 outward remittances fell 2% to $28.98 billion, but money sent for overseas shares and bonds jumped 56% to $2.65 billion ([Pranay Aggarwal on RBI LRS data](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/pranay-aggarwal-_razorpay-already-moved-money-across-borders-activity-7525780603510738944-HLBe)). The consumer's yardstick stays unchanged through all of it: the total cost of a transfer — fee plus FX markup — not the press release announcing the corridor.

## 4. IndusInd rents Mastercard's rails for outbound remittances

Just before the window opened, on November 17, IndusInd Bank and Mastercard announced a collaboration to run outbound cross-border payments from India through Mastercard Move, starting with person-to-person remittances and education-related payments to the US, UK, Europe, Canada and Australia, with SME payments planned next ([Elets BFSI](https://bfsi.eletsonline.com/indusind-mastercard-move-remittances); [Fintech Intel](https://fintech-intel.com/paytech/mastercard-move-and-indusind-bank-collaborate-to-enhance-outbound-cross-border-payments-from-india)). Mastercard Move spans 200-plus countries and territories, over 150 currencies and roughly 17 billion endpoints, letting a bank plug into corridors without building bilateral connections itself ([StartupLanes](https://www.startuplanes.com/post/indusind-bank-partners-with-mastercard-to-streamline-outbound-cross-border-payments)). IndusInd, with about 42 million customers and 3,139 branches as of September 30, 2026, disclosed the arrangement through a stock-exchange filing ([Elets BFSI](https://bfsi.eletsonline.com/indusind-mastercard-move-remittances)).

The significance is that incumbents are responding to the same pain point the Thunes index quantifies — with rented network rails rather than owned corridors. Education payments are the sharpest use case: tuition deadlines are hard, and "end-to-end visibility" for a parent wiring a semester's fees is a genuine improvement over the opaque "initiated, in transit, hope" state most bank remittances offer today. The competitive consequence cuts the other way, though. When a bank's outbound product is Mastercard's plumbing with IndusInd's logo, pricing power sits with the network, and the consumer's ability to compare corridors on total cost depends entirely on how transparently the bank displays fee-plus-FX at initiation. Mastercard Move, Visa Direct and the Wise-style fintechs are converging on the same remittances; that competition only delivers for consumers if the price is shown up front, side by side — which no Indian regulation currently forces on outbound remittances the way it soon will for some inbound products.

## 5. Paytm buys a Southeast Asian beachhead instead of waiting for rails

On November 19, Reuters reported that Paytm's Singapore subsidiary has approved an investment of up to ₹200 crore (about $21 million) in Funding Societies, Southeast Asia's largest SME digital-financing platform — around ₹162 crore of it buying a 19.1% stake in CardUp, the group's digital payments arm, with a five-year technology partnership under which Paytm supplies payments and lending technology to CardUp; the deal is expected to close by May 2027 ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/indias-paytm-partners-with-singapores-funding-societies-plans-up-21-million-2026-11-19); [Marketing Mirrors on the regulatory filing](https://www.facebook.com/Marketingmirrors/posts/paytms-singapore-subsidiary-has-approved-an-investment-of-up-to-200-crore-in-fun/1613425067468501)).

This is the private-sector answer to the question NIPL's whitepaper addresses at the state level: how does an Indian payments company get abroad? Paytm is not waiting for a UPI linkage to make Singapore usable; it is buying distribution (a minority stake keeps it capital-light and keeps regulatory exposure low) and monetising its domestic tech stack as an export in its own right. It is a materially different model from NIPL's — public corridors built by treaty versus private corridors built by cap table — and the two will increasingly coexist. The consumer caveat: CardUp is a B2B payments product, so nothing here moves an Indian traveller's checkout experience in Singapore anytime soon; the near-term beneficiary is Paytm's revenue diversification story as it courts overseas technology-supply contracts. The sector backdrop sharpens the point — with Razorpay preparing IPO papers reportedly seeking around $600 million, cross-border and GIFT City capability has become a listed-company valuation asset, not a side project.

## The through-line: three export strategies, one untested promise

The week put three export strategies side by side. NIPL is buying acceptance abroad (LuLu's capped discounts, Burj Khalifa's ticket counters). NIPL is selling standards abroad (the ADB/EY-Parthenon whitepaper, the Indonesia meetings, the Uzbekistan agreement). And private fintechs are routing around both — Paytm buying a Southeast Asian beachhead, IndusInd renting Mastercard's global rails, Razorpay building from GIFT City. Every one of them rests on the same untested consumer promise: that moving money across India's borders will get faster and cheaper. The October engine behind it all remains immense — a record 25.47 billion UPI transactions worth ₹31.53 lakh crore, with 758 banks live ([Times Kuwait](https://timeskuwait.com/beyond-upi-how-india-is-building-the-next-generation-of-digital-finance)) — but domestic scale does not automatically export price relief. The Thunes–Juniper numbers say Indian cross-border users currently pay the region's highest fees and wait the longest for their money. Watch what a LuLu checkout receipt, an IndusInd tuition remittance and a freelancer's inward wire actually cost end-to-end over the next two quarters. Those three numbers, not the whitepapers, will decide whether UPI's global decade is a consumer story or only a diplomatic one.
