# Fintech Deep Dive — Saturday | November 28, 2026

*Consumer Rights (Complaints, fraud, safeguards) | Developments reported November 21–28, 2026*

This was the week India's fraud-protection debate stopped being about advice and became about controls. The RBI dropped draft directions that would give every bank customer a one-tap "Stop Switch", per-channel kill toggles, self-set transaction limits and an optional "trusted person" gate on large transfers — while rewriting the definition of bank negligence so that missing alerts, office-hours-only fraud helplines and ignored complaints count against the bank. The Supreme Court, hearing the digital-arrest scam case, told the RBI that financial institutions should be required to compensate victims "not less than the amount lost" when a bank's own due-diligence failure lets the fraud through. Delhi Police dismantled a Noida compound where 164 trafficked foreigners were running scripted investment scams on hundreds of devices. CERT-In warned that payment fraud has moved beyond banks to whoever actually processes payouts — including government-linked platforms. And an NBFC learnt that burying rejected complaints instead of escalating them to the Internal Ombudsman carries a price tag. Here is what each development changes for the person actually losing the money.

## 1. RBI's Stop Switch draft: the fraud burden moves from your vigilance to the bank's systems

On November 23, the RBI released draft directions — seven versions, one for each class of bank — titled as amendments to its Responsible Business Conduct framework, with public comments open until December 14, 2026 ([RBI draft for commercial banks](https://www.rbi.org.in/scripts/bs_viewcontent.aspx?Id=5221); [Moneycontrol](https://www.moneycontrol.com/banking/rbi-proposes-stop-switch-to-block-all-outgoing-payments-trusted-person-approval-for-high-value-transfers-article-14087394.html)). The headline feature is the Stop Switch: one action that immediately disables all customer-initiated future debits from an account, while leaving inward credits untouched and carving out existing recurring mandates, standing instructions and certain bank-initiated debits such as loan instalments ([Moneycontrol](https://www.moneycontrol.com/banking/rbi-proposes-stop-switch-to-block-all-outgoing-payments-trusted-person-approval-for-high-value-transfers-article-14087394.html)). Around it sit three more controls: per-channel on/off switches (cards split into domestic and international, across POS, ATM, online and contactless), customer-set limits per transaction, per beneficiary, per day and in aggregate across channels, and a voluntary Trusted Person Mechanism — a nominee aged 18 or above who gets the transaction details and one hour to approve or reject transfers above a threshold the customer sets, with silence counting as rejection ([Moneycontrol](https://www.moneycontrol.com/banking/rbi-proposes-stop-switch-to-block-all-outgoing-payments-trusted-person-approval-for-high-value-transfers-article-14087394.html); [The Indian Express](https://indianexpress.com/article/business/rbi-trusted-person-stop-switch-digital-banking-fraud-rules-10975272)). The TPM deliberately excludes UPI, merchant payments and recurring mandates — the highest-volume flows stay ungated to avoid breaking daily commerce.

The quieter clause is the one with teeth. The draft expands "negligence by a bank" — the trigger that shifts liability to the bank in a fraudulent electronic transaction — to include failure to implement authentication requirements, failure to send mandatory transaction alerts, failure to provide round-the-clock fraud-reporting channels, failure to act promptly on customer complaints, bank system malfunctions, security breaches, and collusion by bank employees or their third-party vendors. The draft's own definition: "Negligence by a bank means any act of omission or commission on part of the bank which results in or contributes to the occurrence of a fraudulent electronic banking transaction in a customer account" ([Moneycontrol](https://www.moneycontrol.com/banking/rbi-proposes-stop-switch-to-block-all-outgoing-payments-trusted-person-approval-for-high-value-transfers-article-14087394.html)). Timelines matter: the negligence clauses bite from January 1, 2027; the rest from July 1, 2027, or earlier if a bank moves first ([CalcGuru on the drafts](https://calcguru.in/tax-updates/rbi-draft-stop-switch-trusted-person-electronic-banking-safeguards)).

Why now? Because banks' default response to fraud claims has been to argue the customer must have authorised the payment. The standard logic — "your UPI PIN was entered, your SIM was bound, therefore you approved it" — was on display in a Telangana State Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission ruling reported through this window: a 44-year-old woman whose ₹55,000 vanished via UPI in November 2022, reported within three days, was told by SBI that PIN entry and SIM-binding proved authorisation or negligence. The Commission rejected that, holding there was no evidence she made the transaction or disclosed credentials, and ordered ₹85,000 — the refund plus compensation and costs — for the bank's failure to refund despite a timely complaint ([Mint](https://www.livemint.com/money/upi-pin-alone-doesnt-prove-transaction-consent-telangana-consumer-commission-orders-sbi-to-pay-85-000-key-details-11795023315198.html); [Financial Express](https://www.financialexpress.com/legal/woman-reports-upi-fraud-within-3-days-but-rs-55000-remained-missing-bank-pays-rs-85000/4388019)). It took four years to get there. The RBI draft attacks the same failure modes from the front (a customer can freeze outflows the moment something feels wrong) and the back (a bank that fumbled alerts, authentication or complaint handling now writes its own negligence finding). The fine print to watch in comments: the stop switch does not cover already-executed mandates, and the one-hour TPM window expires — neither stops a scammer who gets everything done inside sixty minutes.

## 2. Supreme Court pushes banks toward mandatory compensation in digital-arrest scams

The Supreme Court, monitoring the digital-arrest epidemic in *Suo Moto Writ Petition (Criminal) No. 3 of 2025*, has called on the RBI to frame safeguards requiring financial institutions to compensate digital-arrest scam victims "not less than the amount lost" where the fraud is established to have occurred due to the institution's failure to exercise due diligence ([SCC Times](https://www.scconline.com/blog/post/2026/11/25/supreme-court-bank-liability-digital-arrest-scams)). The Court also flagged preventive duties: suspending suspicious transactions mid-flight, alerting the nearest police station, and physically verifying suspicious transfers ([SCC Times](https://www.scconline.com/blog/post/2026/11/25/supreme-court-bank-liability-digital-arrest-scams)). The case file shows the machinery: an Amicus Curiae note dated October 14, a response from the Attorney General on October 28, a hearing on October 29, and directions that also put WhatsApp LLC and other intermediaries on notice to submit concrete preventive proposals rather than relying on passive post-incident takedowns ([King Stubb & Kasiva](https://ksandk.com/newsletter/supreme-court-digital-arrest-cyber-fraud-directions)).

The Court examined the RBI's September draft of the Know Your Customer Amendment Directions, 2026 — the mule-account SOP — slated for notification by November 30, 2026 and operationalisation by December 31, 2026 ([King Stubb & Kasiva](https://ksandk.com/newsletter/supreme-court-digital-arrest-cyber-fraud-directions)). That draft proposes temporary debit holds on suspicious money-mule transactions of ₹1,000 or more, with banks required to notify the account holder within 20 days and a maximum hold of 60 days unless law enforcement directs otherwise ([TaxGuru summary](https://news.vaquill.com/item/4f1adb7b-99e7-4798-9e94-3d29e7c4d31d)). Digital-arrest scams are mule-account logistics: the victim's money fans out through dozens of rented accounts within minutes, so the hold-and-release SOP is an attempt to create a recoverable window without freezing every innocent account that received ₹1,000 from a stranger.

For consumers, two things deserve attention. First, the SC's indemnity framing is stricter than the current liability rules — where a bank's transaction-monitoring or KYC diligence failed, the Court wants compensation at par with the loss, not a negotiated ombudsman award years later. Compliance teams now face a December 31 deadline to wire in anomaly detection and police-intimation workflows ([King Stubb & Kasiva](https://ksandk.com/newsletter/supreme-court-digital-arrest-cyber-fraud-directions)). Second, the burden of proof fight is moving. The liability framework says report fast and you are protected; the Court's direction says the bank must show its own systems worked. Between the two, the "your PIN was entered, case closed" defence — the one the Telangana Commission just rejected — is running out of road.

## 3. Noida: a cyber-slavery compound inside India, staffed by trafficked foreigners

On November 26, Delhi Police's Crime Branch Inter State Cell raided an operation on the outskirts of Delhi and rescued 164 foreign nationals — 92 Indonesians and 72 Malaysians — who police say were lured to India with promises of IT jobs, confined, and forced to run online investment scams against victims abroad ([BBC](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/crkgvjy7q83wo); [Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/indian-police-rescue-dozens-indonesians-malaysians-cyber-fraud-racket-2026-11-27)). A Malaysian national, Tan Guan Wei, 25, was arrested; police recovered 355 mobile phones, 168 desktop computers, 26 laptops, 53 SIM cards and 174 passports, and the rescued workers were trained on written scripts dictating what to say to targets ([The Straits Times](https://www.straitstimes.com/asia/south-asia/india-busts-cyberfraud-human-trafficking-ring-malaysian-arrested-164-foreigners-rescued); [The New Indian Express](https://www.newindianexpress.com/cities/delhi/2026/Nov/27/164-foreigners-saved-from-cyber-trap)). The operation followed an email tip to the Crime Branch about a week earlier ([The New Indian Express](https://www.newindianexpress.com/cities/delhi/2026/Nov/27/164-foreigners-saved-from-cyber-trap)).

The significance is geographic. Southeast Asia's scam compounds — Myanmar, Laos, Cambodia — have been documented for years; this case shows the same industrial model replicated on Indian soil, with India as both base and labour pool. The victims of the investment scams are the consumers whose losses animate the RBI's draft controls and the Supreme Court's indemnity push; the workers inside the compound are themselves trafficking victims, coerced into perpetrating the fraud. Any serious consumer-rights account of digital fraud has to hold both facts at once: the scam economy is a supply chain, and enforcement against one node — the compound, the mule, the app — does not close demand. Police raids in Lucknow and Delhi's Pitampura earlier this year suggest the model is spreading across north India ([BBC](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/crkgvjy7q83wo)). For the fraud victim, what this changes is expectations: recovery odds depend on freezing flows within hours, which is exactly the response window the 1930 helpline system and the proposed stop switch are built around.

## 4. CERT-In: the fraud perimeter has moved beyond banks to whoever pays the vendors

In a critical advisory issued Friday (November 20), CERT-In warned that unauthorised payment transactions now extend beyond banks and financial institutions to companies that process government-linked payments and platforms paying vendors and business partners — attackers are targeting "business records and approval processes" that determine who gets paid ([The Times of India](https://timesofindia.indiatimes.com/india/indias-cyber-security-agency-cert-in-flags-payment-fraud-in-govt-linked-platforms/articleshow/136064992.cms)). The agency separately flagged AI-driven cyberattacks against businesses, government bodies and research institutions, and the warning follows an October advisory on unauthorised transfers in payment and disbursement systems ([The Times of India](https://timesofindia.indiatimes.com/india/indias-cyber-security-agency-cert-in-flags-payment-fraud-in-govt-linked-platforms/articleshow/136064992.cms)). The prescribed hygiene: government bodies that outsource payments must independently verify changes to beneficiary details, payment instructions and approval rights, and providers should keep client systems isolated — with India's standing six-hour incident-reporting clock attached ([The Times of India](https://timesofindia.indiatimes.com/india/indias-cyber-security-agency-cert-in-flags-payment-fraud-in-govt-linked-platforms/articleshow/136064992.cms)).

This matters for consumers in two ways. The direct one: if you are a vendor, freelancer or supplier paid through a government-linked or corporate payout platform, the new attack surface is the approval workflow above you — a changed account number in the master data, and your payment lands in a fraudster's mule account while the payer's records still show compliance. Out-of-band verification before accepting any "update your bank details" request is now official guidance, not paranoia. The structural one: India's fraud-protection architecture — liability limits, ombudsman jurisdiction, the RBI's conduct directions — runs through regulated banks and NBFCs. The entities CERT-In is warning about sit partly outside that perimeter, which means a consumer or small business wronged by a payout-platform breach may discover their grievance has no clean regulatory home. The RBI's conduct framework does not yet name payment aggregators and processors the way it names banks; that gap is worth a consultation response.

## 5. Fusion Finance fined: rejected complaints that never reach the Internal Ombudsman carry a price

On November 17, the RBI imposed a ₹7.50 lakh monetary penalty on NBFC-MFI Fusion Finance Limited for non-compliance with its KYC and Internal Ombudsman directions, disclosed on November 20 ([RBI press release](https://rbi.org.in/scripts/FS_PressRelease.aspx?fn=2766&prid=64015); [Moneylife](https://www.moneylife.in/article/fusion-finance-fined-750-lakh-by-rbi-for-kyc-noncompliance-and-internal-ombudsman-lapses/82246.html)). The statutory inspection, referenced to the company's position as of March 31, 2026, sustained two charges: the company failed to obtain PAN, an equivalent e-document, or Form 60 from customers at account establishment, and it failed to escalate partly or wholly rejected complaints to the Internal Ombudsman for a final decision within the prescribed timeline ([RBI press release](https://rbi.org.in/scripts/FS_PressRelease.aspx?fn=2766&prid=64015); [Moneylife](https://www.moneylife.in/article/fusion-finance-fined-750-lakh-by-rbi-for-kyc-noncompliance-and-internal-ombudsman-lapses/82246.html)). As always, the RBI caveats that the penalty addresses compliance deficiency, not the validity of any customer transaction ([RBI press release](https://rbi.org.in/scripts/FS_PressRelease.aspx?fn=2766&prid=64015)).

The Internal Ombudsman clause is the consumer-relevant half. The IO mechanism exists so that when a regulated entity rejects your complaint — in whole or part — a bank-internal but RBI-overseen reviewer gives it a second look before you must climb to the RBI Integrated Ombudsman. An NBFC that quietly shelves rejected complaints is deleting a rung from the grievance ladder, and customers who never learn the rung existed assume the rejection is final. The penalty amount is small; the signal is not. It lands in the same week the RBI proposed making "failure to act promptly on customer complaints" an explicit species of bank negligence from January 1, 2027 ([Moneycontrol](https://www.moneycontrol.com/banking/rbi-proposes-stop-switch-to-block-all-outgoing-payments-trusted-person-approval-for-high-value-transfers-article-14087394.html)) — the regulator is attacking complaint handling from both ends: escalate internally, or be negligent; and if the internal system fails, the ombudsman fine is the floor. The consumer takeaway is procedural: a rejection is not an answer. Ask for the Internal Ombudsman review, and if the entity stalls, the RBI Ombudsman portal is the next address.

## The through-line: a fraud lifecycle, regulated one end at a time

Read together, the week mapped the full lifecycle of a digital fraud and assigned an institution to each stage. Prevention: the stop switch, channel toggles, limits and trusted-person gate (RBI, comments due December 14, rollout July 2027, negligence rules January 2027). Plumbing: mule-account debit holds with 20-day notice and 60-day caps (KYC amendment, notification due November 30, operational December 31). Production: the Noida compound showing how scams are actually staffed and scripted (police, ongoing). Institutional targets: CERT-In's warning that the perimeter now includes payout platforms and approval workflows (no conduct-direction cover yet). Aftermath: the Supreme Court's indemnity demand and a fine for complaints that never reached the Internal Ombudsman. What the week did not produce is speed — nearly every new protection is dated 2027, while the scams it targets run on hour-scale timelines. Until January, the working playbook stays unchanged: report within the golden hour to 1930 and the bank's fraud channel, put disputes in writing against the June 2026 liability framework, treat every rejection as appealable up to the Internal Ombudsman and RBI Ombudsman, and read the stop-switch draft before December 14 — the comment window is the one moment this rulebook belongs to you.
